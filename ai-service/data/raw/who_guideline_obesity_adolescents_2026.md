## Executive summary

Obesity has important, negative effects on the physical and mental well-being, educational attainment and quality of life of adolescents (1, 2, 3). The World Health Organization (WHO) defines childhood as the period from 0 to 19 years of age. Newborn or neonate refers to the first 28 days of life; infant refers to ages 29 days to less than 12 months; toddler to 12 months to less than 3 years; pre-school-aged to 3 years to less than 5 years; school-aged to 5 to less than 10 years; and adolescent to 10 to 19 years (4, 5, 6). The WHO International Classification of Diseases 11 (ICD-11) defines obesity as “a chronic complex disease defined by excessive adiposity that can impair health. It is in most cases a multifactorial disease due to obesogenic environments, psycho-social factors and genetic variants”. Childhood obesity predisposes the early onset of other noncommunicable diseases and obesity in adulthood (3, 7). With a high prevalence of existing childhood obesity in many communities worldwide, effective measures for obesity management are essential to protect the health of adolescents during these formative and vulnerable periods of life (1, 2, 7).

## Scope and purpose, objectives, and target audience

This guideline aims to provide Member States and their partners with adoptable and adaptable, clear, practice- and science-informed global clinical and public health recommendations on the integrated management and care of adolescents in all their diversity 10 to 19 years of age with obesity for improved health, functioning and reduced obesity-associated disability, using a primary health care approach. Recommendations are grounded in gender equality, equity and human rights, to ensure that no one is left behind. "In all their diversity" recognizes the uniqueness of each adolescent, promoting inclusion, policies, and actions that acknowledge varying needs and experiences. The scope of this work is on primary health care-based interventions, including but not limited to diet therapy; physical activity, sleep hygiene and sedentary behaviours; behaviour changing interventions; multimodal interventions; digital health interventions; pharmacological interventions; and weight-control related surgery. Recommendations consider the perspective of the individual, the community, the multiple sectors involved and the health systems. The primary target audiences include decision-makers and public health policymakers in ministries of health and other offices at the national, sub-national and local levels. This guideline is also aimed at health workers, including providers at the primary care level, integrated health programme managers, and health system managers. The recommendations

are of interest to non-governmental and other organizations as well as professional societies involved in planning and managing interventions for adolescents with obesity, and other health and well-being actions. The guideline can be used to inform the general public, as well as parents and caregivers of adolescents with obesity.

## Guideline development methodology

This guideline adheres to the procedures set out in the WHO Handbook for guideline development (8). The WHO Obesity Steering Committee, led by the Department of Nutrition and Food Safety, oversaw the entire guideline development process. The Guideline Development Group (GDG) included individuals with a range of expertise, skills, and perspectives related to the management of adolescents with obesity. Members represented diversity in sex, age, and geography. A second relevant guideline for obesity management in children was completed following an independent guideline development process. Each guideline had its own GDG. The guideline for obesity management in children can be accessed here. The WHO Obesity Steering Committee drafted an initial list of questions and outcomes for guideline development. This list was subject to a public notice and comment. The results were discussed by the GDG, which approved a final list of questions and outcomes that was then ratified by the WHO Guidelines Review Committee (GRC). Each question was addressed through evidence retrieval, assessment and synthesis carried out according to guidance from WHO (8), Cochrane (9, 10), and GRADE (11). A team of systematic reviewers participated in the guideline development process by presenting evidence and identifying research priorities. The GDG considered additional factors for the implementation of revised obesity management guidelines, including equitable access to health care (12). The outcomes considered by the GDG to be critical for decision-making were 1) body mass index (BMI) /BMI z-score reduction, stabilization and/or maintenance (kg/m), 2) weight reduction, stabilization and/or maintenance (kg), 3) physical well-being (functional mobility, self-care, getting along, life activities and participation, as defined by trialists), 4) mental well-being (anxiety, stress, depression, isolation, self-esteem, body image, suicidal ideation, eating disorders, as defined by trialists), 5) physical activity (type, duration, intensity, frequency, as defined by trialists), 6) overall quality of life, 7) blood pressure, 8) adiposity and fat distribution (objective measures: dual energy x-ray absorptiometry, magnetic resonance, as defined by trialists; anthropometric measures: skinfolds, waist-to-hip ratio, waist circumference, as defined by trialists), 9) adverse events associated with the interventions, 10) presence of obesity-coexisting conditions (any other noncommunicable disease), and 11) obesity-associated disability. The outcomes considered to be important for decision-making were 1) hyperinsulinemia [1.7 mmol/L (30 mg/dL) ] or higher when 1 mg of glucagon is administered intramuscular or intravenous (or as defined by trialists), 2) resistance to insulin (as defined by trialists), 3) glycaemia (in mg/dL, fasting), 4) alterations in lipid metabolism or in adipogenesis and fat accumulation, 5) lipid hormones (for example, leptin or adiponectin), 6) alterations in hunger or satiety (as defined by trialists), 7) reduced disability in any of the functionality domains according to the WHO International Classification of Functioning, Disability and Health (ICF), 8) mortality, 9) prevalence of obesity in adulthood, and 10) access to health services. Outcomes were also informed by evidence on views, values, and preferences of adolescents with obesity, as well as of health workers, family members and community members. The WHO Secretariat presented a comprehensive, objective synthesis of the evidence to the WHO Obesity Steering Committee and the GDG to inform the recommendations. When discussing and deciding on recommendations, the GDG considered the results of the evidence retrieval, synthesis and assessment and of evidence-to-decision frameworks, such as the balance between benefits and harms, stakeholders’ values and preferences, costs, cost effectiveness, equity considerations, acceptability, and feasibility. In addition, they explicitly took into consideration factors of human rights and sociocultural adaptability, societal implications, health system considerations, implementation, monitoring and evaluation, and research gaps. Prior to the meetings, all experts participating in WHO meetings declared any interest relevant to the meeting and potential conflicts were managed accordingly. Participants of the GDG meetings took part in their individual capacity and not as institutional representatives. The final draft guideline was peer-reviewed by an external review group and was subject to public comment and notice. The final version was reviewed by the GDG and ratified by the WHO GRC. Recommendations, justifications and remarks Dietary interventions

## Recommendation

WHO recommends a structured dietary intervention as one of the components of the integrated management of adolescents with obesity. It should consider age, gender, health status, food preferences and meal frequencies, nutritional education, cultural context, and the adolescent’s interests, values and preferences. It should be delivered and supervised by health workers with knowledge on nutrition and involve parents and/or caregivers. Strength of the recommendation: Strong. Quality of the evidence: Very low.

## Justification

The GDG voted for a strong recommendation because the balance between benefits and harms favours dietary interventions. Benefits are small, and vary depending on the type of diet; however, the risk of adverse events is low. Adolescents and their parents or caregivers placed a high value on the potential benefits of dietary interventions for obesity management. Acceptability and feasibility are enhanced when dietary interventions involve peers and family members and when they consider societal and cultural norms. Dietary interventions are likely to be cost-effective because of their potential to prevent obesity and chronic diseases in the long term. However, the GDG also considered that financial constraints may prevent families from affording healthy food choices. The effect on equity is unclear; g ender, culture, socioeconomic status, and parental or caregiver involvement have a direct impact on food insecurity and health disparities. Evidence on dietary interventions was limited in the number and quality of studies. A small effect of dietary treatments for less than six months compared to no dietary changes was identified for the critical outcomes of weight and BMI. Dietary modifications are usually aimed at reducing total energy intake and include, but are not limited to, caloric content restriction (normal or reduced energy intake), varying macronutrient distribution or composition (low carbohydrate, high protein, low fat, omega-3 rich, polyunsaturated fatty acid (PUFA) -rich, or ketogenic), restriction of particular components (low in salt, low in sugar, high in fibre, no food additives, low in industrialized products currently known as ultra-processed

<!-- PDF page 18 -->

foods, or low glycaemic index (GI) foods), and dietary patterns (Mediterranean, American Heart Association, whole foods, complex carbohydrates or time-restricted diet).

## Remarks

Dietary interventions require evaluation by health workers with nutrition knowledge, the inclusion of a physical and mental health evaluation, the family and personal history and environment, and an assessment of risk factors for eating disorders. Health workers should be able to communicate in an empathetic, non-stigmatizing, motivational, and culturally responsive manner, and to display competencies of interdisciplinary collaboration. WHO supports the consumption of a healthy diet to protect against malnutrition in all its forms as well as against noncommunicable diseases. The keys to a healthy diet include breastfeeding during the first two years of age, with exclusive breastfeeding for the first six months of life; consuming a combination of foods while avoiding monotonous diets; eating plenty of fruits, vegetables, and legumes daily; moderating the consumption of fat; and limiting salt and sugar intake. Diets need to be universal in their application, based on human biology and underpinned by evidence. According to the statement by WHO and FAO, a healthy diet needs to be adequate, balanced, moderate and diverse. Individual characteristics (for example age, gender, lifestyle and degree of physical activity), cultural context, locally available foods and dietary customs will determine the exact make-up of a diversified, balanced and healthy diet, although the basic principles remain the same. The guidelines and codes of practice included in Codex Alimentarius standards provide potentially relevant advice regarding nutrient compounds that may be used in special dietary foods for infants and children, formula foods for weight control diets, as well as labelling of and claims of prepackaged foods for dietary uses. Dietary interventions allow school and community involvement in obesity management services by coordinating with caregivers and health workers to provide appropriate foods. This also helps to initiate, support and sustain dietary practices change. Adolescents and their parents or caregivers prefer interventions that are affordable and easy to apply, including a structured meal plan, hands-on activities, and fun approaches to promoting healthy eating habits. Acceptability is enhanced when dietary interventions are tailored to age and cultural patterns. The initiation and follow-up of a diet plan require the participation of the adolescent and their parents or caregivers, services with structured guidance, and consideration of the schedule of parents or caregivers to facilitate compliance and engagement with the intervention. Mental health issues such as anxiety, depression, low self-esteem, and emotional dysregulation may lead to unhealthy eating behaviours. Health workers should address obesity and mental health conditions in the primary health care setting early and together, using inclusive, developmentally appropriate, and family-engaged strategies. Dietary behaviours are influenced by complex factors, including gender, culture, socioeconomic status, and parental and caregiver involvement, with early education and the environment playing crucial roles in long-term health. Low-income communities face higher food insecurity and health disparities, underscoring the need for accessible nutrition resources and better nutritional education. There is a need for assessing risk factors for eating disorders when delivering dietary interventions.

Physical activity, sedentary behaviour, and sleep

## Recommendation

WHO recommends a physical activity plan that considers age, gender, health status, sedentary behaviour, sleeping patterns, cultural context, the adolescent’s interests, values and preferences and the support of parents and/or caregivers as one of the components of the integrated management of adolescents with obesity. Strength of the recommendation: Strong. Quality of the evidence: Very low.

## Justification

The GDG agreed on a strong recommendation because of the desirable outcomes of physical activity for adolescents with and without obesity. While the benefits are modest, so are the potential adverse events. Adolescents with obesity and their parents or caregivers place a high value on staying active, particularly when this involves peers and school activities. Physical activity interventions that are appropriate for the adolescent’s age, gender, and ethnicity and that use people-first language are more acceptable and feasible. The GDG was mindful that while cost-effective, physical activity may incur costs on families. Physical activity will increase equity only if access to physical activity opportunities is provided, particularly for girls and adolescents in low-income settings, with disabilities, or from underserved populations. Evidence on physical activity was limited in the number and quality of studies. A small effect of moderate to vigorous physical activity versus no physical activity was identified for the critical outcomes of BMI, weight loss, body fat, adverse effects, and mental well-being. The 2020 WHO guidelines on physical activity and sedentary behaviour provide physical activity recommendations for adolescents irrespective of gender, cultural background or socioeconomic status (4). The recommendations include: - Adolescents should do at least an average of 60 minutes per day of moderate- to vigorous- intensity, mostly aerobic, physical activity, across the week.

- Vigorous-intensity aerobic activities, as well as those that strengthen muscle and bone,

should be incorporated at least three days a week. The same guidelines recommended that children and adolescents aged 5 to 17 years should limit the amount of sedentary time, particularly the amount of recreational screen time (4).

## Remarks

The inclusion of physical activity as part of a scheduled daily routine that also includes schoolwork, commuting, seeing friends, and leisure could help adolescents to engage in physical activity. Non-competitive activities may be the preferred option for some adolescents. Consider the values and preferences of adolescents and parents/caregivers when deciding how to engage in exercise or physical activity interventions for management of obesity. Structured physical activity plans that take into consideration their motivation require discussion and agreement with the adolescents and caregivers. In general, adolescent girls prefer to engage in physical activity to facilitate weight loss and improve physical appearance, while adolescent boys do so to build muscle and improve athletic performance.

<!-- PDF page 20 -->

Health workers should be equipped with the required competencies and knowledge to facilitate discussion with adolescents and caregivers and promote knowledge on body weight and obesity management. Mental health conditions may result in physical inactivity, sleep disturbances, and social withdrawal. Therefore, health workers should consider both obesity and mental health conditions together using inclusive, developmentally appropriate, and family-engaged strategies. Physical activity plans should be tailored to the specific needs of population groups, including girls, adolescents exposed to exclusion/discrimination, adolescents with disabilities, or people at risk of developing non-communicable diseases. Health workers should consider any underlying impairments that may hinder physical activity engagement in children with disabilities. Enable effective multisector collaborations to create policies supporting the establishment of active environments, for example access to parks, athletic facilities, and recreation areas provided at no cost. Behaviour changing interventions

## Recommendation

WHO recommends behaviour changing interventions for the integrated management of adolescents with obesity. The type and duration of the intervention (s) selected should take into consideration age, gender, health status, physical activity, sedentary behaviour, cultural context, the adolescent’s interests, and values and preferences with the support of parents and/or caregivers. Strength of the recommendation: Strong. Quality of the evidence: Very low.

## Justification

The GDG considered this recommendation to be strong because the balance between benefits and harms probably favours behaviour changing interventions. Adolescents and their parents or caregivers perceive that behaviour changing interventions will lead to long-term, holistic lifestyle changes that go beyond weight loss. Interventions are more likely to be feasible and acceptable if they are solution-based, are delivered by trustworthy health workers, and consider access to facilities and to resources to cover costs. Relatedly, the GDG also acknowledged that behaviour changing interventions may incur costs on families, depending on the care setting, delivery strategy, type of behaviour changing intervention, and health workers involved. Other population groups may also face barriers to access due to stigma, discrimination, exclusion, or lack of appropriately tailored services. WHO has a classification system to design and evaluate behaviour changing interventions in a way that is tailored to different contexts, from individual behaviours to societal-level changes. WHO categorizes interventions by the type of action they promote as, educational/informational interventions, persuasive interventions, normative/structural interventions, environmental interventions, and policy-based interventions, as detailed in the WHO Report on Social Determinants of Health (13), WHO Health Promotion and the 2013 WHO Global Action Plan (14).

<!-- PDF page 21 -->

A non-exhaustive list of behaviour changing interventions includes behavioural therapy, cognitive behavioural therapy, cognitive therapy, psychotherapy, integrative or holistic therapy, self-monitoring, goal setting and reinforcement, stimulus control, problem solving, motivational techniques, mindfulness-based cognitive therapy, or stress reduction.

## Remarks

Adolescents with obesity may be motivated to engage in behaviour changing interventions because of the perceived benefits of such activities, including weight loss, body image, preventing future health problems, self-esteem, and social acceptability. Adolescents with obesity and their caregivers value personalized, holistic, and practical obesity management interventions that promote benefits such as self-esteem, self-confidence, and social acceptability. Behaviour changing interventions are more acceptable when they are tailored to the specific needs of adolescents with obesity, and when they incorporate fun and enjoyable activities that are practical and solution-based. There may be a need for referral to specialized services when there are coexisting mental health issues or when interventions do not lead to progress. Referral should include written notification to other health workers, and clear and compassionate communication with the family regarding the reasons for referral. The cost of behaviour changing interventions may depend on many factors, including the care setting, the type of behaviour changing intervention, and the health workers involved. Addressing the causes of inequities by all society actors is required to enhance participation in obesity management interventions. Support from governments, schools, and the community is likely to facilitate the implementation of behaviour changing interventions. Digital health interventions

## Recommendation

WHO suggests digital health interventions as one of the components of the integrated management of adolescents with obesity. It should consider the type and daily duration of the intervention; the age, gender, health status, physical activity, sedentary behaviour, cultural context and interests, values and preferences of the adolescent, and have the support of parents and/or caregivers. Strength of the recommendation: Conditional. Quality of the evidence: Very low.

## Justification

The GDG agreed on a conditional recommendation because the evidence on digital interventions was limited in the number and quality of studies. Evidence synthesis found a small effect of short- or long-term digital health interventions compared to conventional care alone was identified for the critical outcomes of weight, BMI, and waist circumference.

<!-- PDF page 22 -->

Digital health interventions under the supervision of parents or caregivers could be potentially useful for promoting and sustaining behaviour change over time, although high-quality evidence on effectiveness is limited.

## Remarks

Active video games, exergames, exercise activity applications, pedometers or accelerometers, and other similar interventions may have potential for increasing physical activity and promoting behavioural changes in people with or without accessibility issues or chronic diseases. When deciding on specific digital health interventions, health workers should determine whether there are any coexisting mental health conditions. This is needed to avoid physical inactivity, sleep disturbances, social withdrawal, and other symptoms associated with anxiety, depression, social dysregulation, and other mental health issues. Digital health interventions leading to physical activity and behavioural weight-related modifications are desirable while limiting sedentary screen time (such as watching television, searching the internet, or playing video games). Some guidance, including the Canadian Society for Exercise Physiology 24-Hour Movement Guidelines for the Early Years, indicates that regardless of age, recreational time spent in front of a screen in excess of two hours per day is associated with harmful outcomes such as depression and lower quality of life, and thus this could be used as a daily maximum for screen time. Pharmacotherapy

## Recommendation

WHO suggests pharmacological interventions for obesity management among adolescents 10-19 years of age who have obesity and obesity-related complications* only after a structured and supervised programme of multimodal lifestyle modification** has failed to achieve weight loss, limit weight gain or ameliorate obesity-related complications.

* Obesity-related complications may include type 2 diabetes, hyperinsulinemia, resistance to insulin,

uncontrolled hypertension, alterations in lipid metabolism or in adipogenesis and fat accumulation, and mental health issues. ** The structured and supervised programme of multimodal lifestyle modification should include a personalized behavioural, dietary, and physical activity intervention for at least six months and periodic monitoring of coexisting conditions. Pharmacological interventions should be prescribed in a specialized multidisciplinary setting after close evaluation of physical development and psychological stability, adherence to healthy diet and physical activity habits, closely monitoring potential adverse events and including a structured long-term follow-up. Strength of the recommendation: Conditional. Quality of the evidence: Low.

## Justification

The GDG agreed on a conditional recommendation because while the findings suggest that pharmacological treatments likely provide small to moderate benefits, length of follow up was short and important uncertainties remain regarding adverse effects. The overall quality of evidence was low. The systematic review found studies primarily involving adolescents.

<!-- PDF page 23 -->

Studies were relatively short in duration and mostly in high-income settings. They consistently embedded pharmacotherapy within multimodal care that included lifestyle and behavioural interventions. The field of pharmacotherapy for obesity management is rapidly evolving; new therapeutic options with different action mechanisms, doses, administration routes, and durations of treatment are under assessment for both adults and adolescents. Research focuses on options with increased efficacy, fewer side effects, convenient administration routes (for example, oral as opposed to parenteral), and shorter treatment duration.

## Remarks

For adolescents eligible for pharmacological interventions for obesity management, pharmacotherapy interventions should be delivered as part of a structured and supervised multimodal intervention including behavioural, dietary, and physical activity interventions that consider age, gender, health status, sedentary habits and sleeping patterns, cultural context, the adolescent’s interests, values and preferences. Close evaluation of complete pubertal development, final adult height, and psychological stability are needed when prescribing pharmacological interventions. Prescription should always be in accordance with up-to-date scientific evidence and consider the lack of evidence on long-term follow-up. Pharmacological interventions should be prescribed in a specialized multidisciplinary setting, closely monitoring potential adverse events. Referral from the primary health care setting to specialized care or to a multidisciplinary team may be required. The choice of the appropriate pharmacological intervention should depend on the profile of coexisting conditions and be delivered in keeping with relevant medication indications. Parenteral administration may require training for adolescents and their parents or caregivers. Adolescents with obesity, and their parents or caregivers, should receive continued advice on the potential benefits and adverse events of pharmacotherapy interventions for obesity management. They need to be actively involved in the decision-making process for starting and for discontinuing pharmacological interventions, whether this is due to adverse events, treatment success or failure, or other considerations. Health workers prescribing pharmacological interventions for obesity management should be knowledgeable of the indications, efficacy, adverse effects and potential alternatives if there are adverse events of pharmacological interventions for obesity management. They should also consider possible barriers to treatment, including but not limited to the adolescent’s vulnerability to peer opinion, socio-economic disadvantage, parental support, inability to attend follow-up appointments, and other adherence roadblocks. Access to pharmacological interventions for obesity management may be hampered due to cost, lack of coverage by public and/or private health insurance packages, or logistics (such as transportation or requirements for refrigeration). The introduction of new weight-loss medications in the market may lower treatment costs and improve availability, although costs may still be elevated for users and may require the involvement of health services and insurance providers. Effective multisector collaboration is needed to create policies supporting a continuous, available, accessible, affordable, and safe supply of pharmacological interventions for obesity management, when needed.

Recommendation 1. WHO suggests bariatric surgery for obesity management for adolescents 10-19 years of age with severe obesity (BMI-for-age greater than 4 standard deviations above the WHO Growth Reference median; or BMI-for-age greater than 3 standard deviations above the WHO Growth Reference median and obesity-related complications*) only after a structured and supervised programme of multimodal lifestyle modification** has failed to achieve weight loss, limit weight gain or ameliorate coexisting conditions.

* Obesity-related complications may include type 2 diabetes, hyperinsulinemia, resistance to insulin,

uncontrolled hypertension, alterations in lipid metabolism or in adipogenesis and fat accumulation, and mental health issues. ** The structured and supervised programme of multimodal lifestyle modification should include a personalized behavioural, dietary, and physical activity intervention for at least six months and periodic monitoring of obesity-related complications. Strength of the recommendation 1: Conditional. Quality of the evidence 1: Low. Recommendation 2. WHO recommends that bariatric surgery be prescribed/performed in a specialized multidisciplinary setting after close evaluation of complete pubertal development, final adult height, psychological stability, adherence to healthy diet and physical activity habits. There is also need for closely monitoring of potential adverse events and a structured long-term follow-up. Adolescents should be supported to make informed decisions throughout the intervention. Strength of the recommendation 2: Strong. Quality of the evidence 2: Low. Weight-loss and weight-management devices Due to a lack of evidence, the GDG could not make a recommendation on weight-loss and weight-management devices for obesity management in adolescents. Justification for recommendations on bariatric surgery The GDG agreed on a conditional recommendation because while effects are moderate to large, undesirable effects vary. The WHO-commissioned systematic review found two randomized controlled trials (RCT) and three non-RCTs comparing bariatric surgery with lifestyle interventions. This review found that bariatric surgery is more effective compared to no surgery in reducing BMI and weight, and may lead to improvement in other obesity-related outcomes and quality of life. The GDG considered Recommendation 2 on the need for bariatric surgery to be prescribed/ performed in a specialized multidisciplinary setting after close evaluation of complete pubertal development, because these precautions will decrease the potential risks and adverse events that are associated with bariatric surgery. While adolescents and their parents or caregivers were motivated by the potential for weight loss, they were also concerned about the procedure itself and the potential complications.

## Remarks

As a general approach, bariatric surgery for obesity management in adolescents must be delivered as part of a structured and supervised multimodal intervention that includes behavioural, dietary, and physical activity interventions that consider age, gender, health status, existing disability, sedentary habits and sleeping patterns, cultural context, the adolescents’ interests, values and preferences, and that involves parents and/or caregivers. Health workers need to assess potential genetic, hereditary, and familial factors when considering bariatric surgery. The choice of bariatric surgery will depend on a patient’s profile and must be delivered in accordance with medical indications and the adolescent’s values and preferences when appropriate. Prior to the intervention, continued advice needs to be provided to adolescents with obesity and their parents or caregivers on the potential benefits and adverse events of bariatric surgery for obesity management. Support and advice should continue after surgery. Multimodal interventions

## Recommendation

WHO recommends a structured multimodal intervention including a combination of diet, physical activity, and behavioural components for the integrated management of adolescents with obesity*. It should consider age, gender, health status, food preferences, nutrition education, physical fitness, cultural context, and the adolescent’s interests, values and preferences as well as resources and support of parents and caregivers. The intervention should be delivered and supervised by health workers with knowledge on nutrition, physical activity and behaviour changing interventions.

* Other components of multimodal interventions include changes in sleep behaviour and in sedentary

behaviour patterns, and the use of digital technologies. Multimodal interventions could involve family, friends, caregivers and schools, could improve access to care, and promote awareness and educational campaigns for adolescents, caregivers, schools and communities. Strength of the recommendation: Strong. Quality of the evidence: Very low.

## Justification

The GDG reached consensus for a strong recommendation because adolescents with obesity, and their parents, value the perceived benefits of multimodal interventions, including weight loss, prevention of health sequelae, and improved self-esteem, body image and social desirability. While benefits are small and the potential adverse events are unknown, the GDG agreed that benefits likely outweigh the harms. Multimodal interventions are acceptable and feasible, particularly when they include fun, informal (non-competitive) activities and incorporate technology. The GDG was mindful that costs will depend on the specific aspects of the intervention; unequal access may exacerbate obesity and poor health outcomes among marginalized communities and those of low socio-economic status. The WHO-commissioned review retrieved RCTs of multimodal interventions consisting of dietary, physical activity, and behaviour modifications, compared to usual care delivered either in healthcare settings or in community settings such as schools, churches, and community centres. Results were similar in both settings, including small effects on BMI z-scores at different endpoints, BMI, physical well-being, and quality of life.

## Plans for updating

The WHO Secretariat will monitor the development of research in the area of obesity. This will include emerging evidence, emerging considerations and interests from stakeholders and/or users of the guideline, changes in values and preferences, acceptability, feasibility, or cost-effectiveness of interventions; considerations of human rights, equity, and gender; issues surrounding the coverage of interventions; emerging definitions or cut-off points for defining obesity and severity; the role of new technologies and social media; and the emerging need for recommendations for specific populations, including adolescents with obesity-related complications, migrant populations, or minorities, among others. WHO will work to determine whether new evidence or considerations warrant revisiting or updating specific recommendations. Updates will be incorporated to the overall guideline; changes will be highlighted and disseminated via the regular communication channels of WHO. ## Background

he International Classification of Diseases 11 (ICD-11) defines obesity as “a chronic complex disease defined by excessive adiposity that can impair health. It is in most cases a multifactorial T disease due to obesogenic environments, psycho-social factors and genetic variants. In a subgroup of patients, single major etiological factors can be identified (medications, diseases, immobilization, iatrogenic procedures, monogenic disease/genetic syndrome). Body mass index (BMI) is a surrogate marker of adiposity calculated as weight (kg) /height² (m²). The BMI categories for defining overweight vary by age and gender in infants, children and adolescents.” (1). In addition to the ill health and disability caused directly by excess weight gain, obesity is linked to a range of diseases and short- to long-term health outcomes (2). These include asthma, cancer, cardiovascular diseases, dementia, depression, diabetes mellitus, dysfunction in fertility and impotence, kidney disease, liver disease, osteoarthritis, pancreatitis, birth complications and sleep apnoea (3). Many children with obesity continue to have obesity as adolescents and adults (4). Obesity may be coupled with underlying impairments that, in combination with personal and environmental factors, can lead to disability. Underlying impairments related to obesity, such as physical limitations due to excess body weight, symptoms associated with chronic conditions, and mental health issues such as anxiety and depression, may result in further unwanted consequences such as stigma, discrimination, and lack of access to health care, thus generating or exacerbating disability and obesity-associated disability (2, 5). The World Health Organization (WHO) defines childhood as the period from 0 to 19 years of age, with the following breakdown of age ranges: newborn or neonate (the first 28 days of life); infant (29 days to less than 12 months of life); toddler (from 12 months to less than 3 years; of age); preschool-aged (from 3 to less than 5 years); school-aged (from 5 to less than 10 years;) and adolescent (from 10 to 19 years) (6, 7, 8). For children under 5 years of age, overweight is de fi ned as weight-for-height greater than 2 standard deviations (SD) above the median of WHO Child Growth Standards, and obesity is defined as weight-for-height greater than 3 SD above that same median (3). For children and adolescents aged between 5 and 19 years, overweight is de fi ned as BMI-for-age greater than 1 SD above the median, and obesity as BMI-for-age greater than 2 SD above the median (3). Adolescence is a unique stage of human development and an important time for laying the foundations of good health. Adolescents experience rapid physical, cognitive and psychosocial growth. This affects how they feel, think, make decisions, and interact with the world around them (9).

<!-- PDF page 30 -->

Globally, the rates of obesity have nearly tripled since 1975, affecting both adults and children (10). In 2022, more than 2.5 billion people aged 18 years and older were overweight, and over 890 million adults had obesity (12). The global age-standardised prevalence of obesity among persons 5 to 19 years of age increased between 1990 and 2022 from 1.7% and 2.1 in girls and boys to 6.9% and 9.3%, respectively (12). In 2022, the global crude obesity prevalence among children and adolescents was about 8%, with sharp increases occurring over the past 20 years (12, 13). A 2024 pooled analysis reported that, in 2021, 93.1 million children and adolescents between 5 and 14 years of age, and 80.6 million adolescents and young adults 15 to 24 years of age, had obesity, affecting approximately 160 million children and adolescents worldwide. This number is predicted to increase to more than 250 million by 2030 (11). Furthermore, the double burden of underweight and obesity is an increasing problem in many countries. There are varying obesity rates within countries, mostly related to determinants such as poverty, education, and access to health care (12). Regional differences in prevalence estimates are important to note, since the highest rates of childhood obesity occur in North America (~17%) and the Caribbean (~19%), Central America (~16%), and Polynesia (~20%) (13). In contrast, obesity was lower in Central Africa (~2%), Eastern and Western Africa (~4%), and Southern Africa (~6%) as well as in Western, Eastern and Northern Europe (~5%) (13). There is no consensus on the reasons behind increases in obesity rates during the past decades, although diet, physical activity and personal behavioural choices have been repeatedly highlighted. Lack of appropriate multisectoral and policy efforts have hampered efforts to stem obesogenic environments, address drivers of obesity, and remove blame from people with obesity and their families (14). Furthermore, people’s choices regarding food, physical activity, and consumption practices are deeply influenced by commercial determinants of health, for example private sector actions around the production of industrialized products currently known as ultra-processed foods, marketing of unhealthy commodities, and the promotion of sedentary lifestyles (15). WHO has long advocated for, among other actions, enacting marketing restrictions for breast-milk substitutes, and food and beverage marketing that targets children and adolescents (16). A recent synthesis of evidence revealed that combining dietary and physical interventions has a limited effect on preventing obesity in children and adolescents. While the risk of serious adverse events appears to be low, the overall quality of evidence is also low (17, 18, 19). Economic and technological changes have resulted in a shift in diets, from subsistence and local food production to commercial food production and a subsequent increase in marketing of industrialized products currently known as ultra-processed foods. This in turn has led to an increase in caloric intake, in the consumption of sugar and processed-foods and of animal-source foods, particularly in low-income countries. It has also led to a decrease in time spent preparing food and a lower consumption of fibre-rich and fresh foods (12, 20, 21). Physical activity in children and adolescents leads to enhanced bone and skeletal health, cardiometabolic health, and the development of cognitive and motor skills. In adults, physical activity also reduces the risk of obesity and non-communicable diseases. Worryingly, as of 2016, 81% of adolescents 11 to 17 years of age do not meet the recommended levels of physical activity (22, 23). In addition, longer periods of screen time have led to increased sedentary behaviour and mental health conditions, both of which are linked to obesity (6, 7). Individual choices about food and physical activity are influenced by societal and environmental conditions. It is important to recognize this, since obesity tends to be linked to an individual’s responsibility and actions. While multimodal interventions have the potential to lead to increased physical activity and modified dietary intake (24), tackling obesity requires multisectoral actions that have a direct impact on access to healthy foods and to physical activity venues, on poverty reduction and urban planning, and on food manufacturing, marketing and pricing (25).

Rationale In order to respond to current global epidemiology, and to complement existing obesity prevention guidelines, normative guidance from WHO is needed focusing on the people-centred integrated management and care of adolescents in all their diversity. This must be done using a primary health care approach, which has three components: 1) meeting people’s health needs throughout their lives; 2) addressing the broader determinants of health through multisectoral policy and action; and 3) empowering individuals, families and communities to take charge of their own health (26). "In all their diversity" recognizes the uniqueness of each adolescent, promoting inclusion, policies, and actions that acknowledge varying needs and experiences (27, 28). Guidelines for managing obesity also need to consider the developmental stages of children and adolescents. The main considerations include differences in the diagnosis and prognosis of obesity, the role of parents and caregivers, behavioural aspects and mental health, eligibility for interventions, and values and preferences. To address this need, WHO has developed the present obesity management guideline, and another specifically for children. That guideline, which can be accessed here, followed its own independent guideline development process with a separate Guideline Development Group (GDG), in accordance with the WHO Handbook for guideline development (29). The present guideline aims to provide practice- and science-informed global clinical and public health recommendations to improve case management and care pathways for adolescents with obesity. The recommendations refer to interventions delivered by multidisciplinary teams that provide seamless, coordinated care within the primary care setting and via referrals to secondary and tertiary care (30). Recommendations considered a people-centred approach, that is, consciously incorporating the views, values and preferences of those affected by the recommendations (31), an intentional commitment to compassionate care (32, 33, 34), and strategies to ensure that family and communities are empowered to improve health practices (35) and to care for their own health (26). Guideline development acknowledged individual differences such as gender, nationality, race, ethnicity, ability, sexual orientation, socio-economic status, religious beliefs, political beliefs, and other ideologies (27, 28) and highlighted related research gaps. The guideline is committed to equity in the form of justice, equality, impartiality and fairness in service provision. This guideline was built upon knowledge from existing WHO and UN guidance. Previous clinical guidelines have focused on approaches to preventing overweight and obesity (together), including assessing and managing children and adolescents in the primary care setting. The WHO Commission on Ending Childhood Obesity recommended to “develop and support appropriate weight management services for children and adolescents who are overweight or obese that are family-based, multicomponent (including nutrition, physical activity and psychosocial support) and delivered by multiprofessional teams with appropriate training and resources, as part of Universal Health Coverage” (4). WHO’s commitment to health equity was reflected in the establishment of the Commission on Social Determinants of Health in 2005 (36), the Global Strategy on Equity and Universal Health Coverage in 2018 (37), and the integration of these aspects into the WHO guideline development methodology (29). There is a need to step away from a one-size-fits-all approach and instead consider specific socioeconomic, cultural and geographic contexts. Guideline development should further acknowledge underlying causes of obesity, for example poverty, education and access to health care. It is also key to take into account differences based on gender, age, nationality, race, ethnicity, ability, socio-economic background, religion, political belief, or other identities (38). Scope and purpose, objectives, and target audience Scope and purpose This guideline focuses on adolescents 10 to 19 years of age with obesity. The outcomes are primary health care-based interventions, including but not limited to diet therapy or therapeutic diets; physical activity, sleep hygiene and sedentary behaviours; behaviour changing interventions; multimodal interventions; digital health interventions; pharmacological interventions; and weight-control surgery. Recommendations concern the individual, the community, health systems and multiple relevant sectors. Outcomes were informed by evidence on the perspectives, views, values, and preferences of adolescents with obesity as well as those of health workers, family members and community members. Objectives The purpose of this guideline is to provide Member States with adoptable and adaptable, clear, practice- and science-informed clinical and public health recommendations on the integrated management and care of adolescents with obesity for improved health, functioning and reduced obesity-associated disability, using a primary health care approach. Recommendations are grounded in gender equality, equity and human rights, to ensure that no one is left behind. The specific objectives are to: a. Provide recommendations on individual, family, community and home-based interventions for the integrated management of adolescents with obesity. b. Outline practice and science-informed strategies for managing adolescents with obesity, for their caregivers and the home environment to support health, well-being, functioning, the healthier achievement and maintenance of weight goals, and reduced obesity-associated disability. c. Provide guidance on monitoring treatment, health and well-being outcomes, including dietary habits and healthier behaviours and lifestyles.

Guideline development Interventions included in this guideline for evidence search and recommendations Dietary interventions: An approach to creating combinations of calories, macro and/ or micronutrients for obesity management. Dietary approaches and food composition depend primarily on the adolescent’s age, weight loss goal, coexisting conditions and food preferences and habits (51, 52). Physical activity: Any bodily movement produced by skeletal muscles that requires energy expenditure (53, 54, 55). Regular physical activity can prevent and contribute to the management of type 2 diabetes, heart disease, hypertension, some types of cancer, and other chronic diseases (54, 55). The 2020 WHO guidelines on physical activity and sedentary behaviour provide physical activity recommendations for people irrespective of gender, cultural background or socioeconomic status (6, 7). Behaviour changing interventions: Interventions designed to effect individual behaviour change (56, 57). They play a role in obesity management by addressing the energy-balance behaviours that contribute to weight gain, and that hamper obesity management efforts. While there is no consensus on a taxonomy or classification, behaviour changing interventions may include (i) interventions that invoke behavioural models or theory; (ii) interventions based on psychotherapeutic models such as behavioural therapy, cognitive behavioural therapies, mindfulness, attitude and relationship techniques, psychotherapy, psychodynamic therapies, humanistic therapies, stimulus control, goal setting, and self-monitoring; and (iii) multimodal interventions, which are composite approaches to modifying behaviour using more than one component (56, 57). Multimodal interventions: Therapeutic approaches that incorporate a combination of different methods, strategies, or modes of treatment. Instead of relying on a single approach, multimodal interventions utilize a variety of components, for example behavioural and educational interventions, dietary management, and encouragement to increase physical activity and reduce sedentary behaviour. In this guideline, pharmacological and/or bariatric surgery interventions were not considered as components of multimodal interventions (58).

<!-- PDF page 36 -->

Digital health interventions: A discrete functionality of digital technology that is applied to achieve health objectives. Digital health interventions may be implemented within digital health applications, and information and communication technology systems, including communication channels (59). They include the use of wearable or implantable devices to monitor diet and physical activity, websites, and text messages or other texting. There are also mobile applications to deliver information on diet and physical activity and monitor behaviours. Other interventions include exergaming or active video gaming to promote physical activity, as well as telemedicine to provide counselling and monitor interventions. Pharmacological interventions: These aim to reduce energy intake and/or increase energy expenditure. They may be a suitable course of action for obesity management when lifestyle interventions have failed and there are serious coexisting conditions that affect physical and mental well-being (60). However, patients need to be closely monitored for potential adverse events (61). Currently, the United States Food and Drug Administration (FDA) and the European Medicines Agency (EMA) have approved orlistat, semaglutide, and liraglutide for the treatment of obesity in adolescents. The FDA has also approved phentermine and phentermine/topiramate. These pharmacological interventions are to be administered as an adjunct to diet, physical activity, and other lifestyle modifications (6 2). Metabolic and bariatric surgery: Surgical procedures to manage obesity and obesity-related conditions. The rationale of metabolic and bariatric surgery is to restrict food intake and reduce the absorption of food. While surgery may be an option given its long-term efficacy outcomes, not all patients are eligible, particularly adolescents with obesity (6 3). Different types of procedures are available, including Roux-en-Y gastric bypass, adjustable banding, and sleeve gastrectomy. Of these, laparoscopic Roux-en-Y gastric bypass is the most commonly conducted procedure in adolescents (64, 65, 66). Weight-loss or weight-management devices: Minimally invasive interventions for obesity management. They include weight-loss devices such as intragastric balloons, electrical stimulation, gastric emptying systems; and weight-maintenance devices such as removable oral palatal space-occupying devices, and ingested, transient, space-occupying devices (67, 68). Principles and existing documents related to this new guideline The 2016 WHO Commission on Ending Childhood Obesity highlighted the need for recommendations on multimodal obesity management interventions delivered by multiprofessional teams with appropriate training and resources, as part of Universal Health Coverage (4). The search for evidence aimed to comprise studies from all WHO regions, in any language, not limited to the six official UN languages. The search strategy and results were documented and reported, including the reasons for having excluded specific studies. In addition to intervention reviews, the search covered qualitative evidence syntheses, rapid reviews and overviews on costs. It also considered issues pertaining to the values and preferences of children and adolescents with obesity, family members, carers and health care providers. The WHO Secretariat identified review teams through a competitive Call for Authors. This Call was distributed via: 1) the WHO website; 2) the nutrition and food safety mailing list (the Campaign Monitor software reaches over 13 000 subscribers); and 3) the WHO LinkedIn account. In addition, members of the WHO Obesity Steering Committee distributed it through their networks. All review teams members completed Declaration of interest and Confidentiality forms.

In addition to considerations on the search and analysis of evidence (29, 69, 70), the following principles guided the development of recommendations: Primary health care approach: A whole-of-society approach to health that aims to maximize the level and distribution of health and well-being through three components: 1) primary care and essential public health functions as the core of integrated health services; 2) addressing the broader determinants of health through multisectoral policy and action; and 3) empowering individuals, families and communities to take charge of their own health. The function of primary health care is to coordinate the care of people and their health needs over the life course and across the continuum of care, in primary care facilities and communities, and for individuals and populations. Care needs to be comprehensive, coordinated, people-centred, continuous and accessible (26, 7 1). Integrated Management of Childhood Illness (IMCI): IMCI includes three components: 1) improving case management skills of health workers, 2) improving overall health systems, and 3) improving family and community practices (3 9, 7 2). In health care facilities, IMCI entails the identification of illness in outpatient settings, and adopting a people-centred, integrated care perspective rather than focusing on a specific illness. It also entails working with caregivers, and expediting the referral of severely ill children to the appropriate facility or specialist in the health system or network of service providers to address the relevant health needs (32). People-centred care: An approach to care that purposely adopts the perspectives of individuals, carers, families and communities as participants in and beneficiaries of trusted health systems that respond to their needs and preferences in humane and holistic ways. People-centred care also requires that people have the education and support they need to make decisions and participate in their own care (33). Compassionate care: A principled and intentional effort to understand a person, discern their needs, and alleviate their suffering through relational understanding and action. Compassionate care in obesity management requires a competency-based approach where health workers adopt non-judgmental and non-stigmatizing language, attitudes, knowledge, and skills that cover the multiple domains of compassion, as well as assessments by or feedback from patient’s families or peers (34, 35, 36). Disability: The experience of any condition that makes it more difficult for a person to do certain activities or have equitable access within a given society. Disabilities may be cognitive, developmental, intellectual, mental, physical, sensory, or a combination of multiple factors (27, 28). Equity: Equity refers to a condition in which society is characterized by justice, equality, impartiality and fairness, including fair and equal distribution of power, economic resources, opportunities, goods and services across the social spectrum. In an equitable society people are not excluded from the activities of society, such as education, employment, or health care and there is no bias or favouritism (27, 28). Gender equality: All persons, regardless of their gender, should enjoy the same status in society; have the same entitlements to all human rights; enjoy the same level of respect in the community; can take advantage of the same opportunities to make choices about their lives; and have the same amount of power to shape the outcomes of these choices (27, 28). Diversity: Refers to the existence of differences among people with respect to age, class, ethnicity, gender, health, physical and mental ability, race, sexual orientation, religion, physical size, education level, job and function, personality traits, and other human differences (27, 28). ## Recommendations

he GDG acknowledged that the strong recommendations below were based on low- or very low-quality evidence, thus resulting in discord between the quality of evidence and the T corresponding strength of the recommendation. The GDG reached consensus and voted for strong recommendations based on factors included in the evidence-to-decision frameworks that reach beyond the evidence on effects. This was because obesity is a multifactorial disease that requires a multimodal approach to address all possible converging factors, since one or more of them can impact individuals at different times or simultaneously. The GDG considered the practicality and universality of adopting diet, physical activity, and behavioural approaches as single or as multimodal interventions, as they are pillars for an adequate approach to obesity management. According to the WHO Handbook for guideline development (29), a strong recommendation is based on the confidence that the desirable effects associated with a recommendation outweigh the undesirable effects. Most people would want the recommended intervention, and only a small proportion would not. Furthermore, the recommendation can be adopted as policy and as a quality or performance indicator. For a conditional recommendation, there is less certainty on the balance between the desirable and undesirable effects. While many people would want the recommended intervention, many others would not. Health workers should be aware that different courses of action will be appropriate for different individual patients, who will need support for making decisions that are consistent with their values and preferences. As such, developing policy will require debate and input from different stakeholders. Dietary interventions Summary of evidence WHO commissioned a Cochrane systematic review on dietary interventions for adolescents with obesity. The review included evidence from mixed study populations (2 to 21 years of age) and from adolescent-only study populations (10 to 19 years of age) (51). Authors report that the benefit of dietary interventions on body weight, BMI, and waist circumference varied depending on the types of dietary intervention. The quality of evidence ranged from very low to moderate (Annex 2. Dietary interventions).

## Recommendation

WHO recommends a structured dietary intervention as one of the components of the integrated management of adolescents with obesity. It should consider age, gender, health status, food preferences and meal frequencies, nutritional education, cultural context, and the adolescent’s interests, values and preferences. It should be delivered and supervised by health workers with knowledge on nutrition, and involve parents and/or caregivers. Strength of the recommendation: Strong. Quality of the evidence: Very low.

## Justification

The GDG voted for a strong recommendation because the balance between benefits and harms favours dietary interventions (Annex 3. Evidence-to-decision considerations for dietary interventions). Benefits are small, and they vary depending on the type of diet; however, the risk of adverse events is low. Adolescents and their parents or caregivers placed a high value on the potential benefits of dietary interventions for obesity management. Acceptability and feasibility are enhanced when dietary interventions include peers and family members and when they consider societal and cultural norms. Dietary interventions are likely to be cost-effective because of their potential to prevent obesity and chronic diseases in the long term. However, the GDG also considered that financial constraints may prevent families from affording healthy food choices. The effect on equity is unclear; gender, culture, socioeconomic status, and parental or caregiver involvement have a direct impact on food insecurity and health disparities (93) (Annex 3. Evidence-to-decision considerations for dietary interventions). Evidence on dietary interventions was limited in the number and quality of studies. A small effect of dietary treatments for less than six months compared to no dietary changes was identified for the critical outcomes of weight and BMI (51). Dietary modifications are usually aimed at reducing total energy intake and include, but are not limited to, caloric content restriction (normal or reduced energy intake), varying macronutrient distribution or composition (low carbohydrate, high protein, low fat, omega-3 rich, PUFA rich, or ketogenic), restriction of particular components (low in salt, low in sugar, high in fibre, no food additives, low in industrialized products currently known as ultra-processed foods, or low GI foods), and dietary patterns (Mediterranean, American Heart Association, whole foods, complex carbohydrates or time-restricted diet).

## Remarks

Dietary interventions require evaluation by health workers with nutrition knowledge, the inclusion of a physical and mental health evaluation, the family and personal history and environment, and an assessment of risk factors for eating disorders (76, 81). Health workers should be able to communicate in an empathetic, non-stigmatizing, motivational, and culturally responsive manner, and to display competencies of interdisciplinary collaboration. WHO supports the consumption of a healthy diet to protect against malnutrition in all its forms as well as against noncommunicable diseases. The keys to a healthy diet include breastfeeding during the first two years of age, with exclusive breastfeeding for the first six months of life; consuming a combination of foods while avoiding monotonous diets; including plenty of fruits, vegetables, and legumes daily; moderating the consumption of fat; and limiting salt and sugar intake. The GDG agreed on a conditional recommendation because while the findings suggest that pharmacological treatments likely provide small to moderate benefits, length of follow up was short and important uncertainties remain regarding adverse effects. ## Recommendation

WHO recommends a structured multimodal intervention including a combination of diet, physical activity, and behavioural components for the integrated management of adolescents with obesity*. It should consider age, gender, health status, food preferences, nutrition education, physical fitness, cultural context, and the adolescent’s interests, values and preferences as well as resources and support of parents and caregivers. The intervention should be delivered and supervised by health workers with knowledge on nutrition, physical activity and behaviour changing interventions.

* Other components of multimodal interventions include changes in sleep behaviour and in sedentary

behaviour patterns, and the use of digital technologies. Multimodal interventions could involve family, friends, caregivers and schools, could improve access to care, and promote awareness and educational campaigns for adolescents, caregivers, schools and communities. Pharmacotherapy and bariatric surgery for adolescents need to follow the corresponding recommendations included in this guideline development process. Strength of the recommendation: Strong. Quality of the evidence: Very low.

## Justification

The GDG reached consensus for a strong recommendation because adolescents with obesity and their parents value the perceived benefits of multimodal interventions, including weight loss, prevention of health sequelae, and improved self-esteem, body image and social desirability (Annex 3. Evidence-to-decision considerations for multimodal interventions). While benefits are small and the potential adverse events are unknown, the GDG agreed that benefits likely outweigh the harms. Multimodal interventions are acceptable and feasible, particularly when they include fun, informal (non-competitive) activities and incorporate technology. The GDG was mindful that costs will depend on the specific aspects of the intervention; unequal access may exacerbate obesity and poor health outcomes among marginalized communities and those of low socio-economic status (Annex 3. Evidence-to-decision considerations for multimodal interventions). The WHO-commissioned review retrieved RCTs of multimodal interventions consisting of dietary, physical activity, and behaviour modifications, compared to usual care delivered either in health care settings or in community settings such as schools, churches, and community centres. Results were similar in both settings, including small effects on BMI z-scores at different endpoints, BMI, physical well-being, and quality of life (58).

## Remarks

The recommendations on diet, physical activity and behavioural components, established during the process of developing this guideline, could form the basis of a multimodal intervention. This guideline includes recommendations on sleep behaviour, sedentary behaviour, digital technologies, and the need to involve family, friends, caregivers, schools and the community. The specific components of the intervention can be decided based on the results of a detailed initial clinical assessment that considers anthropometric measures as well as a comprehensive family and medical history, a review of psychosocial and behavioural factors (such as eating habits, physical activity, sleep routines), and relevant laboratory tests where appropriate (76).

<!-- PDF page 69 -->

Parental or caregiver involvement can enhance the effectiveness of lifestyle interventions. Parents or caregivers can support the adolescent throughout the intervention, change their own behaviours, and adapt parenting styles. Multimodal interventions may be cost-effective due to potential benefits in weight management and prevention of chronic diseases. Referral from the primary health care setting to specialized care may be required, including notification of other health workers and clear, compassionate communication with the family (76). Adolescent obesity experts, secondary or specialized care, and/or the involvement of multidisciplinary teams in multimodal lifestyle interventions could be an option. The frequency of following up adolescents with obesity should consider overall clinical condition and coexisting conditions, medication use, and social vulnerability (76). Whole-of-society approaches that address the causes of inequities in access are required to enhance participation in obesity management interventions (3). Support from governments, schools, and the community could facilitate the implementation of multimodal interventions.

## Research gaps

Research on multimodal interventions in adolescents should prioritize well-designed and well- reported RCTs and prospective controlled cohort studies that report efficacy, effectiveness, safety profile, long-term secondary effects, weight regain, and cost. Multimodal interventions need to consider the specific barriers that adolescents face in order to prevent inequities in obesity health outcomes. Studies are needed to analyse the cost-effectiveness of multimodal interventions for adolescents with obesity. Studies are also needed on the cost or resources required to implement multimodal interventions in adolescents with obesity.

Annex 1. Background questions Genetic or familial factors

## Question 1. Are there genetic, hereditary, and familial factors that need to be considered when

managing adolescents with obesity? Obesity is a complex phenotype arising from genetic, environmental, and developmental (foetal programming) interactions. Approximately 135 loci, many concentrated in 12 to 15 genes, show consistent and reproducible associations with common obesity in children and adolescents (74). Many of these variants are also associated with adult obesity, suggesting that genetic contributions to body composition are relatively stable across the life course. The most extensively studied genes include the FTO (fat mass and obesity-associated) gene (especially SNP rs9939609, associated with modest increases in BMI and replicated across European, Asian, and Amerindian populations), MC4R, and others frequently involved in appetite and energy regulation, such as TMEM18, SEC16B, ADCY3, BDNF, and NEGR1 (74). Although gene-gene interactions and genetic risk scores have been investigated, findings remain inconclusive. These findings suggest that obesity is a polygenic trait, with numerous small-effect variants acting alongside environmental influences (74). To date, DNA methylation is the most extensively studied mechanism, often in blood or cord blood, with some studies identifying CpG sites associated with later adiposity (75). Circulating microRNAs (miRNAs) also showed differential expression in childhood obesity, though findings varied due to heterogeneity in methods and tissues used. There are significant gaps in understanding how environmental exposures, particularly endocrine- disrupting chemicals (EDCs), shape epigenetic changes linked to obesity risk (75). Initial Clinical Assessment

## Question 2. What are the components of an initial clinical assessment* when managing

adolescents with obesity?

* Besides anthropometry and physical examination, an initial clinical assessment may include

family and psycho-social history, behaviour, initial clinical assessment and biochemical parameters. For adolescents with overweight or obesity, taking a thorough medical history is essential for guiding care and building rapport with the adolescent and their caregiver. It should be respectful, age- appropriate, and culturally sensitive, using open-ended questions and including the adolescent in the discussion. Consent ought always to be asked of both the adolescent and the parent or caregiver before undertaking any assessment in relation to weight. The history should explore specific risk factors related to obesity. These include dietary habits (portion sizes and frequency of consuming junk food and sugary drinks), physical activity levels, and screen time (76). A family history of obesity, type 2 diabetes, or maternal gestational diabetes should be assessed, along with psychosocial stressors and prolonged corticosteroid use. The health worker should also ask about symptoms related to potential causes or complications of obesity, such as headaches (suggesting hypertension), sleep disturbances, joint pain, bullying, depression, low self-esteem, binge-eating, irregular menses or signs of polycystic ovary syndrome, polyuria/polydipsia (suggesting diabetes), and abdominal pain (suggesting cholelithiasis) (76).

<!-- PDF page 92 -->

A complete physical examination is essential and should take into consideration the adolescent’s comfort using appropriate physical space, including larger equipment (for example an exam table, a chair, and blood pressure cuffs). The exam should include a systematic assessment of the skin, head and neck, respiratory, cardiovascular, abdominal, musculoskeletal, neurological, and developmental systems, as well as growth measurements and vital signs. Special attention should be given to measuring blood pressure to assess for hypertension, evaluating pubertal stage, and inspecting for acanthosis nigricans—dark, velvety patches in skin folds—indicating resistance to insulin. Signs of endocrine disorders such as Cushing syndrome should also be considered, including central obesity, moon face, buffalo hump, easy bruising, and pink striae. Growth parameters (including BMI, waist circumference and other anthropometric measures), developmental milestones, and behavioural cues should be reviewed in a compassionate manner, with appropriate referrals made when specialized assessments or investigations are needed (76). Gender- or sex-specific considerations

## Question 5. Are there any gender- or sex-specific aspects that need to be considered when

managing adolescents with obesity in the primary health care setting? There are important gender- and sex-specific considerations that should be addressed when managing adolescents with obesity in the primary health care setting. Evidence shows that sex influences the prevalence and health outcomes of obesity, as well as adolescents’ experiences of stigma, help-seeking behaviour, and how they engage with health care. Girls, for instance, are more likely to internalize weight-related stigma, report lower self-esteem, and experience body dissatisfaction, which may contribute to disordered eating or avoidance of physical activity. Meanwhile, boys may be less likely to seek help due to norms around masculinity and may underreport emotional struggles associated with weight (33, 77, 78). Health workers in primary care should be equipped to recognize these patterns and apply a gender- responsive approach. This includes using non-stigmatizing, non-judgemental and inclusive language, creating safe spaces for adolescents to express body image concerns, and tailoring interventions that are culturally and socially appropriate (33, 77, 78). Sex and gender often overlap with other social factors such as poverty, disability, ethnicity, and sexual identity. For instance, girls in food-insecure households may have less access to nutritious food, and adolescents with disabilities may face structural barriers to physical activity or health care due to barriers in the surrounding environment. Therefore, managing adolescent obesity effectively requires a holistic, equity-oriented approach that respects adolescents and strengthens inclusive, person- centred care in the primary health care setting (3 1, 77, 78). Disability-specific considerations

## Question 6. Are there any disability*-specific aspects that need to be considered when managing

adolescents with obesity in the primary health care setting?

* Disability refers to the outcome of the interaction between health conditions and impairments

that an adolescent might have with personal and environmental factors. Recognizing the relationship between obesity and disability is essential. Obesity can worsen functional limitations and medical complications, while some disabilities inherently increase the risk of weight gain. Addressing both conditions in an integrated, equitable way is key to improving outcomes for adolescents with complex care needs. Adolescents with physical, intellectual, or developmental disabilities face a significantly higher risk of obesity compared to their peers without disabilities. This elevated risk stems from overlapping factors, including but not limited to reduced opportunities for physical activity, dietary challenges (such as feeding difficulties, sensory sensitivities, or limited access to nutritious food), side effects of medication, and social exclusion (32, 79, 80). ## Question 7. What are the approaches for treating and/or co-managing mental health conditions*

in adolescents with obesity? Should considerations be explored regarding how mental health issues can exacerbate obesity or vice versa, and if so, starting at what age?

* WHO conceptualizes mental health as a “state of well-being in which the individual realizes

his or her own abilities, can cope with the normal stresses of life, can work productively and fruitfully, and is able to make a contribution to his or her community”. Mental health conditions include but are not limited to eating disorders, anxiety, stress, depression, isolation, suicidal ideation, weight-based stigmatization and victimisation, low self-esteem and poor body image. For adolescents, mental health care is a critical component of effective obesity management and should be explored at every stage of care. Adolescents with obesity are at increased risk of experiencing depression, anxiety, body dissatisfaction, social isolation, bullying, and low self-esteem. These experiences can reinforce unhealthy behaviours such as emotional eating, avoidance of physical activity, or substance use, all of which may further contribute to weight gain. At the same time, existing mental health conditions can impair motivation, executive function, and adherence to treatment, making obesity more difficult to manage (81). Primary health care providers should adopt integrated, person-centred approaches that address both physical and emotional health. This includes regular mental health screening during obesity care, the use of non-stigmatizing communication, and referral pathways to specialized psychological support when needed. Effective interventions include cognitive behavioural therapy, group support programmes, stress management strategies, and caregiver involvement in treatment planning (81). Importantly, mental health services should be inclusive and responsive to the diverse identities and needs of adolescents, including those with disabilities, neurodevelopmental conditions, and who come from marginalized or gender-diverse backgrounds. These adolescents may face multiple forms of stigma that intensify mental health challenges and impact access to care. Safe, confidential, and adolescent-friendly health settings are vital to encourage help-seeking and sustained engagement. The WHO-UNICEF Helping adolescents thrive toolkit emphasizes that mental health promotion should be part of routine adolescent health care, especially among those with chronic conditions such as obesity. To be effective, these efforts must consider local mental health determinants, risk factors, and access barriers (81).

## Question 8. What is the competency-based training (required knowledge, attitude and skills) that

primary health care workers should receive to manage adolescents with obesity for improved health, functioning and obesity-associated disability? Primary care settings play a vital role in the early identification, management, and coordination of care for children and adolescents with obesity. To support health workers in delivering effective, patient-centred care, a set of core competencies has been proposed. These are based on a review of existing literature on the roles and training needs of primary health care workers (82), and are organized into four key domains: 1. Medical knowledge, patient care, and procedural skills: Emphasizes accurate use of BMI charts, percentiles and growth charts, early recognition of obesity-related coexisting conditions, and adherence to evidence-based treatment guidelines. Health workers should provide developmentally appropriate care and refer patients to specialists when necessary. 2. Interpersonal and communication skills: Highlights the use of empathetic, non-stigmatizing communication, motivational interviewing, and culturally responsive counselling. It also stresses the importance of engaging families, recognizing social determinants of health, and connecting patients with community-based supports. 3. Practice-based learning, improvement, and professionalism: Underscores the need to incorporate obesity-specific competencies into pre-service education and continuing professional development. Training should address provider bias, developmental considerations, and include regular assessment of competency. 4. Systems-based practice: Promotes interdisciplinary collaboration, timely referrals, and integration of obesity care into clinical workflows and electronic health records. It calls for enabling policies, sustainable resources, and scalable, context-sensitive strategies to support implementation across diverse health systems. Together, these skills equip health workers in primary care to: (1) assess and manage obesity in a clinically sound and developmentally appropriate manner; (2) communicate effectively to support patient and family engagement; (3) collaborate with specialists to coordinate comprehensive care; and (4) pursue continuous learning and improvement to maintain high-quality obesity care (82).
