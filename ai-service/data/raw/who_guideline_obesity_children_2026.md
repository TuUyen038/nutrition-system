## Executive summary

Obesity has profound effects on the physical and mental well-being of children (1, 2, 3). The World Health Organization (WHO) defines childhood as the period of life from birth until 19 years of age. Within this group, newborn or neonate refers to the first 28 days of life; infant refers to age 29 days to less than 12 months; toddler to 12 months to less than 3 years; pre-school-aged to 3 to less than 5 years; school-aged to 5 to less than 10 years; and adolescent to 10 to 19 years (4, 5, 6). The WHO International Classification of Diseases 11 (ICD-11) defines obesity as “a chronic complex disease defined by excessive adiposity that can impair health. It is in most cases a multifactorial disease due to obesogenic environments, psycho-social factors and genetic variants”. With increasing prevalence rates of childhood obesity across Member States, there is a need for practice- and science-informed measures to address this condition and its effects on overall health, the early onset of other noncommunicable diseases, educational attainment, adult obesity, and quality of life (1, 2, 7).

## Scope and purpose, objectives, and target audience

The purpose of this guideline is to provide Member States and their partners with adoptable and adaptable, clear, practice- and science-informed global clinical and public health recommendations on the integrated management and care of children in all their diversity, aged 29 days to under 10 years living with obesity, for improved health, functioning and reduced obesity-associated disability, using a primary health care approach. "In all their diversity" recognizes the uniqueness of each child, promoting inclusion, policies, and actions that acknowledge varying needs and experiences. This guideline considered primary health care-based interventions, particularly diet therapy; physical activity, sleep hygiene and sedentary behaviours; behaviour changing interventions; multimodal interventions; digital health interventions; pharmacological interventions; and weight-control related surgery, among others. The guideline development process considered the perspective of the individual, the community and the multiple sectors involved in addition to the health systems. The primary target audiences of this guideline are decision-makers and public health policymakers in ministries of health and other related offices at the national, sub-national and local levels. In addition, the guideline is of interest to health workers and to managers of integrated health programmes and healthcare systems. Furthermore, the recommendations may be informative for non-governmental and other organizations and professional societies

## Guideline development methodology

This guideline was developed following the methodological procedures described in the WHO Handbook for guideline development (8). The Department of Nutrition and Food Safety and the WHO Obesity Steering Committee were responsible for coordinating the guideline development process. The Guideline Development Group (GDG) assessed the results of evidence synthesis and participated in drafting recommendations. The GDG members represented diverse sex, age, and geographic backgrounds with different academic and professional expertise related to childhood obesity management. A second relevant guideline for obesity management in adolescents was completed through a separate, independent process, also following the WHO Handbook. Each guideline had its own GDG. The guideline for obesity management in adolescents can be accessed here. A preliminary set of questions and outcomes was defined by the WHO Obesity Steering Committee; these were then subject to a public notice and comment. The results of this consultation exercise allowed the GDG and the WHO Obesity Steering Committee to refine the preliminary list of questions and outcomes, which was then ratified by the GDG and the WHO Guidelines Review Committee (GRC). WHO commissioned the retrieval, assessment and synthesis of evidence for each question. These exercises were completed in accordance with methodological guidance from WHO (8), Cochrane (9, 10), and GRADE (11). In order to identify systematic reviewers, WHO launched a call for authors. These authors conducted the reviews and presented results to the WHO Secretariat and the GDG. Findings served as the basis for making recommendations, along with factors such as views, values, and preferences of children with obesity and their families, equitable access to health care, costs, acceptability, and feasibility (12). The GDG determined that the following outcomes were critical for decision-making: 1) body mass index (BMI) /BMI z-score reduction, stabilization and/or maintenance (kg/m), 2) weight reduction, stabilization and/or maintenance (kg), 3) physical well-being (functional mobility, self-care, getting along, life activities and participation, as defined by trialists), 4) mental well-being (anxiety, stress, depression, isolation, self-esteem, body image, suicidal ideation, eating disorders, as defined by trialists), 5) physical activity (type, duration, intensity, frequency, as defined by trialists), 6) overall quality of life, 7) blood pressure, 8) adiposity and fat distribution (objective measures: dual energy x-ray absorptiometry, magnetic resonance, as defined by trialists; anthropometric measures: skinfolds, waist-to-hip ratio, waist circumference, as defined by trialists), 9) adverse events associated with the interventions, 10) presence of obesity coexisting conditions (any other noncommunicable disease), and 11) obesity-associated disability. The GDG determined that the following outcomes were important but not critical for decision- making: 1) hyperinsulinemia [1.7 mmol/L (30 mg/dL) ] or higher when 1 mg of glucagon is administered IM or IV (or as defined by trialists), 2) resistance to insulin (as defined by trialists), 3) glycaemia (in mg/dL, fasting), 4) alterations in lipid metabolism or in adipogenesis and fat accumulation, 5) lipid hormones (for example, leptin or adiponectin), 6) alterations in hunger or satiety (as defined by trialists), 7) reduced disability in any of the functionality domains according to the International Classification of Functioning, Disability and Health (ICF), 8) mortality, 9) prevalence of obesity in adulthood, and 10) access to health services.

The WHO Secretariat presented a comprehensive, objective synthesis of the evidence to the WHO Obesity Steering Committee and the GDG to inform the recommendations. When discussing and deciding on recommendations, the GDG considered the results of evidence retrieval, synthesis and assessment and of evidence-to-decision frameworks, for example the balance between benefits and harms, stakeholders’ values and preferences, costs, cost effectiveness, equity considerations, acceptability, and feasibility. In addition, they explicitly took into consideration factors of human rights and sociocultural adaptability, societal implications, health system considerations, implementation, monitoring and evaluation, and research gaps. Prior to their participation, all experts in WHO meetings declared any interest relevant to the meeting and potential conflicts were managed accordingly. Participants in the GDG meetings took part in their individual capacity and not as institutional representatives. An external group of experts was tasked with providing feedback to the final draft of the manuscript, which was then reviewed and approved by the GDG and the WHO GRC. Recommendations, justifications and remarks Dietary interventions

## Recommendation

WHO recommends a structured dietary intervention as one of the components of the integrated management of children with obesity older than 2 years of age. It should consider age, gender, health status, food preferences and meal frequencies, nutritional education, cultural context, and be delivered and supervised by health workers with knowledge on nutrition, and involve parents and/or caregivers. Strength of the recommendation: Strong. Quality of the evidence: Very low.

## Justification

The GDG agreed on a strong recommendation because the balance between benefits and harms probably favours dietary interventions. Children with obesity and their carers positively valued the potential benefits of dietary interventions, which are likely to be acceptable and feasible, particularly when they involved the entire family unit and were personalized, age- appropriate and culturally sensitive. While benefits are small, so is the risk of adverse events. The GDG, on the other hand, acknowledged that financial constraints may hinder parents from affording healthy food or meal plans; socioeconomic status was linked to diet quality, with low-income and historically marginalized communities facing higher food insecurity and health inequities. No studies were found on cost-effectiveness. Evidence on dietary interventions was limited in the number and quality of studies. A small effect of dietary treatments, that is, low calorie or low glycaemic index (GI) food diets compared to no dietary changes, was identified for critical outcomes of weight, BMI, waist circumference, cholesterol concentrations, and percent body fat. Dietary modifications are usually aimed at reducing total energy intake and include, but are not limited to, restricting caloric content (normal or reduced energy intake), varying macronutrient distribution or composition (low carbohydrate, high protein, low fat, omega-3 rich, polyunsaturated

<!-- PDF page 18 -->

fatty acid (PUFA) rich, or ketogenic diet), restricting particular components (low in salt, low in sugar, high in fibre, no food additives, low in industrially produced foods currently known as ultra-processed foods, or low GI foods), and dietary patterns (Mediterranean, American Heart Association, whole foods, or complex carbohydrates).

## Remarks

Dietary interventions require evaluation by health workers with nutrition knowledge. They should cover physical and mental health, family and personal history and environment, and an assessment of risk factors for eating disorders. Health workers should be able to communicate in an empathetic, non-stigmatizing, motivational, and culturally responsive manner, and to display competencies of interdisciplinary collaboration. WHO supports the consumption of a healthy diet to protect against malnutrition in all its forms as well as against noncommunicable diseases. The keys to a healthy diet include breastfeeding during the first two years of age with exclusive breastfeeding for the first six months; consuming a combination of foods avoiding monotonous diets, including plenty of fruits, vegetables, and legumes daily; moderating fat consumption; and limiting salt and sugar. Dietary interventions allow school and community involvement and coordination with caregivers and health workers to provide appropriate foods. This also helps to initiate, support and sustain dietary practices change. According to WHO and FAO, a healthy diet needs to be adequate, balanced, moderate and diverse. The exact make-up of a diversified, balanced and healthy diet will vary depending on individual characteristics (for example age, gender, lifestyle and degree of physical activity), cultural context, locally available foods and dietary customs. In addition, the Codex Alimentarius standards provide potentially relevant guidelines and codes of practice regarding nutrient compounds that may be used in special dietary foods for infants and young children, formula foods for weight control diets, and labelling of and claims of prepackaged foods for dietary uses. Children and their parents or caregivers prefer interventions that are affordable and easy to apply, including a structured meal plan, hands-on activities, and fun approaches to promoting healthy eating habits. Acceptability is enhanced when dietary interventions are tailored to age and cultural patterns. Diet plan initiation and follow-up require the involvement of the children and their parents or caregivers, services with structured guidance, and consideration of the schedule of parents or caregivers to facilitate compliance and engagement with the intervention. Anxiety, depression, low self-esteem, emotional dysregulation, and other mental health issues may lead to unhealthy eating behaviours. Health workers should address both obesity and mental health conditions early and together, using inclusive, developmentally appropriate, and family-engaged strategies. Dietary behaviours are influenced by complex factors, including gender, culture, socioeconomic status, and parental involvement, with early education and the environment playing crucial roles in long-term health. Low-income communities face higher food insecurity and health disparities, underscoring the need for accessible nutrition resources and better nutritional education.

Physical activity, sedentary behaviour, and sleep

## Recommendation

WHO recommends a structured physical activity plan that considers age, gender, health status, cultural context, and the child’s interests, values and preferences and that includes parents and/or caregivers as one of the components of an integrated management for children with obesity. Strength of the recommendation: Strong. Quality of the evidence: Very low.

## Justification

The GDG voted for a strong recommendation because although small, the benefits of physical activity are clear for all ages and health statuses, with trivial to small adverse events. Children with obesity and their parents value the benefits of staying active. The GDG also considered that physical activity interventions are acceptable and feasible, particularly when they include fun, informal, non-competitive activities. Physical activity may be cost-effective due to the potential benefits of preventing chronic diseases, and it can be offered at low or no cost in parks and recreation areas or as lifestyle changes. The recommendation was agreed to be strong even though structured physical activity may result in costs to families; it also requires investment from governments and communities in venues and facilities. Gains in health equity will only crystallize if access to safe, affordable, and suitable programmes and spaces for physical activity are ensured for all, particularly girls, minoritized groups, and people with disabilities. Evidence was limited in the number and quality of studies on children with obesity. No clear effect of physical activity compared to no physical activity was identified for the critical outcomes of BMI, weight loss, body fat, adverse effects, quality of life and mental well-being. The 2020 WHO guidelines on physical activity and sedentary behaviour for children under 5 years of age provide physical activity recommendations for children irrespective of gender, cultural background or socioeconomic status (5). The recommendations include:

- Infants (under 1 year of age) should be physically active several times a day in a variety of

ways, particularly through interactive floor-based play; more is better. For those not yet mobile, this includes at least 30 waking minutes in a prone position (tummy time) spread throughout the day. - Children 1–2 years of age should spend at least 180 minutes in a variety of physical activities at any intensity, including moderate- to vigorous-intensity physical activity, spread throughout the day; more is better. - Children 3–4 years of age should spend at least 180 minutes in a variety of physical activities at any intensity, of which at least 60 minutes is moderate- to vigorous-intensity physical activity, spread throughout the day; more is better. - Children 5 years of age and above should do at least an average of 60 minutes per day of moderate- to vigorous-intensity, mostly aerobic, physical activity, across the week.

- Vigorous-intensity aerobic activities, as well as those that strengthen muscle and bone,

should be incorporated at least three days a week for children 5 years of age and above.

## Remarks

Incorporating physical activity into a scheduled daily routine that also includes schoolwork, commuting, seeing friends, and leisure could help children to engage in physical activity. Non- competitive activities could be the preferred option for some children. The structured physical activity plan should be discussed and agreed upon with the child and their parents or caregivers. Health workers need to be equipped with the required services and competencies to facilitate discussion with children and parents or caregivers and promote knowledge on body weight and obesity management. Mental health issues may contribute to physical inactivity, sleep disturbances, and social withdrawal. There is a need to address both obesity and mental health conditions in the primary health care setting through inclusive, developmentally appropriate, and family-engaged strategies. The structured physical activity plan should be tailored to the specific needs of population groups, including girls, children exposed to exclusion/discrimination, children with disabilities, or people at risk of developing non-communicable diseases. Children with disabilities may require personalized attention regarding their underlying impairments and how they may hinder physical activity. Effective multisectoral collaboration is needed to develop policies supporting the establishment of active environments, for example, access to parks, athletic facilities, and recreation areas at no cost. Behaviour changing interventions

## Recommendation

WHO recommends behaviour changing interventions as one of the components of the integrated management of children with obesity. The type and duration of the intervention (s) selected should take into consideration age, gender, health status, dietary behaviour, physical activity, sedentary behaviour, cultural context, the child's interests, and values and preferences as well as the support and resources of parents and/or caregivers. Strength of the recommendation: Strong. Quality of the evidence: Very low.

## Justification

The GDG agreed on a strong recommendation because children and their families are motivated by the expectation that behaviour changing interventions will help them achieve long-term lifestyle changes. Benefits are small, but so is the possibility of adverse events. Behaviour changing interventions that are fun, enjoyable, and consider the specific needs of participants are more likely to be acceptable and feasible. When reaching this decision, the GDG took into consideration that families may incur costs depending on the components of the interventions and the logistics and health workers involved. Other population groups may also face barriers to access due to stigma, discrimination, exclusion, or lack of appropriately tailored services.

<!-- PDF page 21 -->

WHO has a classification system to design and evaluate behaviour changing interventions in a way that is tailored to different contexts, from individual behaviours to societal-level changes. WHO categorizes interventions by the type of action they promote as educational/informational interventions, persuasive interventions, normative/structural interventions, environmental interventions, and policy-based interventions, as detailed in the WHO Report on Social Determinants of Health (13), WHO Health Promotion and the 2013 WHO Global Action Plan (14). A non-exhaustive list of behaviour changing interventions includes behavioural therapy, cognitive behavioural therapy, cognitive therapy, psychotherapy, integrative or holistic therapy, self-monitoring, goal setting and reinforcement, stimulus control, problem solving, motivational techniques, mindfulness-based cognitive therapy, or stress reduction.

## Remarks

Children with obesity may be motivated to engage in behaviour changing interventions because of the perceived benefits of such activities, including weight loss, body image, prevention of health sequelae, self-esteem, and social acceptability. Children with obesity and their caregivers value personalized, holistic, and practical obesity management interventions that promote benefits such as self-esteem, self-confidence, and social acceptability. Behaviour changing interventions are more acceptable when they are tailored to the specific needs of children with obesity and when they incorporate fun and enjoyable activities that are practical and solution-based. There may be a need for referral to specialized services when there are coexisting mental health issues or when interventions do not lead to progress. Referral should include written notification to other health workers and clear and compassionate communication with the family regarding the motivation for the referral. The cost of behaviour changing interventions may depend on many factors, including the care setting, the type of behaviour changing intervention, and the health workers involved. Whole-of-society approaches addressing the causes of inequities in access are required to enhance participation in obesity management interventions. The implementation of behaviour changing interventions could be facilitated by governments, schools, and community support. Digital health interventions

## Recommendation

WHO suggests a digital health intervention as one of the components of the integrated management of children over 2 years of age with obesity. It should consider the type and daily duration of the intervention, age, gender, health status, physical activity, sedentary behaviour, cultural context, the child’s interests, values and preferences, and be conducted under the supervision of parents and/or caregivers. Strength of the recommendation: Conditional. Quality of the evidence: Very low.

<!-- PDF page 22 -->

## Justification

The GDG agreed on a conditional recommendation because the evidence on digital interventions was limited in the number and quality of studies. The evidence highlights that digital health interventions under parent or caregiver supervision could be potentially useful for promoting and sustaining behaviour change over time. A small effect of short- or long-term digital health interventions compared to conventional care alone was identified for the critical outcomes of weight, BMI, and waist circumference. Digital health interventions under the supervision of parents or caregivers could be potentially useful for promoting and sustaining behaviour change over time, although high-quality evidence on effectiveness is limited.

## Remarks

Supervised active video games, exergames, exercise activity mobile applications, pedometers or accelerometers, and other similar interventions may have potential for increasing physical activity behaviours in children older than 2 years of age with or without accessibility issues or chronic diseases. Health workers should be mindful of coexisting anxiety, emotional dysregulation, and other mental health issues when deciding on digital health interventions in order to avoid sleep disturbances, social withdrawal, and other symptoms associated with mental health conditions. While active video games, exergames, or exercise activity mobile applications may be suggested for managing obesity in children older than 2 years of age, recreational time spent in front of a screen (TV, video games, computers, mobile phones) in excess of two hours a day is associated with harmful outcomes such as depression and lower quality of life. As a result, some guidance, including the Canadian Society for Exercise Physiology 24-Hour movement guidelines for the early years, point out that two hours may be used as a daily maximum for screen time. Pharmacotherapy

## Recommendation

WHO does not recommend pharmacological interventions for obesity management for children 0–9 years of age. Strength of the recommendation: Strong. Quality of the evidence: Low.

## Justification

The GDG agreed on a strong recommendation because the WHO-commissioned Cochrane systematic review of randomized controlled trials (RCTs) assessing the effect of any form of pharmacological interventions compared to no intervention or placebo for obesity in children and adolescents did not identify any studies that included children under 6 years of age. Eight studies included children 6 years or older; seven assessed metformin and one assessed liraglutide. This recommendation accounts for the lack of evidence on both benefits and the potential adverse events and consequences of the different medications and/or their active ingredients on normal growth, development, physiological parameters, and mental health.

## Remarks

As a general approach for children with obesity, a structured and supervised multimodal lifestyle intervention including behavioural, dietary, and physical activity interventions that considers age, gender, health status, sedentary patterns and sleeping habits, cultural context, the child’s interests, values and preferences, and that involves parents and/or caregivers should be considered. Bariatric surgery and weight-loss and weight-management devices

## Recommendations

WHO does not recommend bariatric surgery and weight-loss or weight-management devices for obesity management for children 0-9 years of age. Strength of the recommendation: Strong. Quality of the evidence: No evidence.

## Justification

The GDG agreed on a strong recommendation without relevant evidence by considering the potential adverse events and consequences of bariatric surgery and weight-loss or weight- management devices on normal growth, development, physiological parameters, and mental health. When deciding upon the strength of this recommendation, the GDG considered GRADE guidance regarding paradigmatic situations where a strong recommendation may be warranted despite low or very low confidence in the evidence. In this case, bariatric surgery at such young age, that is under 10 years, may result in significant harm (15).

## Remarks

As a general approach for children with obesity, a structured and supervised multimodal lifestyle intervention including behavioural, dietary, and physical activity interventions that considers age, gender, health status, sedentary patterns and sleeping habits, cultural context, the child’s interests, values and preferences, and that involves parents and/or caregivers should be considered.

Multimodal interventions

## Recommendation

WHO recommends a structured multimodal intervention including a combination of diet, physical activity, and behavioural components for the integrated management of children with obesity*. It should consider age, gender, health status, food preferences, nutritional education, physical fitness, cultural context, and the child’s interests, values and preferences as well as resources and support of parents and caregivers. It should be delivered and supervised by health workers with knowledge on nutrition, physical activity, and behaviour changing interventions, and involve parents and/or caregivers.

* Other components of multimodal interventions include changes in sleep behaviour and in

sedentary behaviour patterns, and the use of digital technologies. Multimodal interventions could involve family, friends, caregivers and schools; improve access to care; and promote awareness and educational campaigns for children, caregivers, schools and communities. Strength of the recommendation: Strong. Quality of the evidence: Very low.

## Justification

The GDG considered this recommendation to be strong because children with obesity and their parents value the perceived benefits associated with a healthy diet and staying active, including weight loss, social acceptance, and self-esteem. Even though the benefits are small, the balance between benefits and harms still favours the intervention. Multimodal interventions are likely to be acceptable and feasible if they are appropriate for age, gender, and ethnicity; if they are personalized; and if they acknowledge parental financial situation and schedules. Relatedly, the GDG acknowledged that multimodal interventions may incur costs on families and that the effect on equity is unclear. Underserved communities, girls, and minoritized ethnic groups are less likely to have access. The WHO-commissioned review retrieved RCTs of multimodal interventions delivered either in health care settings or in community settings, for example, schools, churches, and community centres. Interventions consisted of dietary, physical activity, and behavioural modifications compared to usual care. Results were similar in both settings, including small effects on BMI, physical well-being, and quality of life.

## Remarks

The recommendations on diet, physical activity and behavioural components established during this guideline development process could form the basis of a multimodal intervention. This guideline includes recommendations on sleep behaviour, sedentary behaviour, digital technologies, and the need for involving family, friends, caregivers, schools and the community. The nature of the intervention can be decided based on the results of a detailed initial clinical assessment that considers anthropometric measures as well as a comprehensive family and medical history, a review of psychosocial and behavioural factors (such as eating habits, physical activity, and sleep routines), and relevant laboratory tests where appropriate.

<!-- PDF page 25 -->

Parental involvement can enhance the effectiveness of lifestyle interventions. Parents can support the child throughout the intervention, change their own behaviours, and adapt parenting styles. Multimodal interventions may be cost-effective due to potential benefits in weight management and the prevention of chronic diseases. Referral from the primary health care setting to specialized care may be required, including notification of other health workers and clear, compassionate communication with the family. The involvement of paediatric obesity experts, secondary or specialized care, and/or multidisciplinary teams in multimodal lifestyle interventions could be an option. While there is no ideal follow-up interval for children with obesity, the frequency of visits should consider the child’s overall clinical condition, coexisting conditions, medication use, and social vulnerability. Whole-of-society approaches addressing the causes of inequities in access are required to enhance participation in obesity management interventions. Support from governments, schools, and the community could facilitate the implementation of multimodal interventions.

## Research gaps

There is a need for high-quality evidence on the long-term benefits and harms of obesity management interventions among children. This will require well-designed and well-reported RCTs and prospective cohort studies. In addition to efficacy, study designs should consider and report the duration of treatment, the continued engagement, setting, safety profile, and long-term secondary effects. These studies should incorporate, when appropriate, subgroup analyses for the severity of obesity, obesity-related complications, differently-abled people and type of intervention. Future research should include a varied range of outcomes that go beyond anthropometric and cardiometabolic variables to include physical and mental well-being, overall quality of life, and a reduction in obesity-associated disability. Potential short- and long-term adverse events should be carefully recorded and reported. Intervention design should foresee stakeholders’ values and preferences and foster the involvement of parents, schools, and peers. Future research should also consider implementation enablers for middle- and low-income countries, as well as population characteristics such as gender, age, ethnicity, cultural background, and socio-economic status. Criteria that facilitate decision making in health care, such as the costs, acceptability, and feasibility of interventions, should be taken into account and reported.

## Plans for updating

This guideline follows a living guideline methodology. Future iterations will incorporate new evidence regarding benefits and adverse events, values and preferences from stakeholders and/ or users of the guideline; feasibility or cost-effectiveness of interventions; human rights, equity, and gender considerations; accessibility and public and private coverage of the interventions; definitions or cut-offs points for defining obesity and severity; digital health interventions and social media; and the needs of children with obesity-related complications, of migrant populations, and of minorities. The WHO Secretariat will monitor the development of relevant research development as well as suggestions from stakeholders regarding new evidence or additional questions or outcomes that could be incorporated. Changes will be highlighted in the updated version of the guideline, which will then be disseminated via the regular communication channels of WHO.

## Background

hildren with obesity are likely to experience poor physical and mental well-being, quality of life, and educational achievement. Many communities worldwide currently face a C high prevalence of childhood obesity. Effective management strategies are crucial to safeguarding the health of children and adolescents throughout these vulnerable and formative stages of life. Furthermore, childhood obesity increases the risk of developing other noncommunicable diseases early on, and contributes to obesity persisting into adulthood (1, 2). According to the World Health Organization (WHO) definition, childhood is the life period spanning from 0 to 19 years of age. Newborn or neonate refers to the first 28 days of life; infant refers to 29 days to less than 12 months of age; toddler to 12 months to less than 3 years; pre- school-aged to 3 to less than 5 years; school-aged to 5 to less than 10 years; and adolescent to 10 to 19 years (3, 4, 5). WHO thresholds for childhood obesity vary according to age. In children under 5 years old, overweight is identified as weight-for-height greater than two standard deviations (SD) above the median, and obesity as weight-for-height greater than three SD above the same median (6). For children and adolescents aged 5 to 19 years, overweight is defined as a body mass index (BMI) for-age greater than one SD above the median of the WHO Child Growth Standards, while obesity is classified as a BMI-for-age exceeding two SD above that median (6). The International Classification of Diseases 11 (ICD-11) characterizes obesity as “a chronic complex disease defined by excessive adiposity that can impair health. It is in most cases a multifactorial disease due to obesogenic environments, psycho-social factors and genetic variants. In a subgroup of patients, single major etiological factors can be identified (medications, diseases, immobilization, iatrogenic procedures, monogenic disease/genetic syndrome). Body mass index (BMI) is a surrogate marker of adiposity calculated as weight (kg) /height² (m²). The BMI categories for defining overweight vary by age and gender in infants, children and adolescents. ” (7). Obesity is associated with a wide range of diseases and both short- and long-term health consequences. Children who experience obesity continue to struggle with the condition into adolescence and adulthood (1). Cardiovascular diseases, asthma, cancer, dementia, depression, diabetes mellitus, kidney and liver diseases, osteoarthritis, pancreatitis, birth complications, sleep apnoea, and fertility dysfunction including impotence are recognized as obesity-associated conditions (6). Obesity is associated with underlying impairments that, in combination with personal and environmental factors, may result in disability. In and of itself, disability is a risk factor for childhood obesity due to reduced physical capability, lack of understanding on the part of carers, withdrawal, and health issues. Furthermore, underlying impairments related to obesity, such as physical limitations due to excess body weight, symptoms associated with chronic conditions,

<!-- PDF page 28 -->

and mental health issues such as anxiety and depression, may result in further unwanted consequences such as stigma, discrimination, and lack of access to health care, thus generating or exacerbating disability and obesity-associated disability (8, 9). Since 1975, global rates of obesity have nearly tripled, affecting both children and adults. In 2016, over 1.9 billion people aged 18 years and older were classified as overweight, and more than 650 million adults were living with obesity (6). According to 2021 WHO data, the global prevalence of overweight or obesity among children and adolescents, 5 to 19 years of age rose from 4% to 18% (6). Additional studies confirm increasing childhood obesity rates. A 2024 pooled analysis reported that 93.1 million children and adolescents 5 to 14 years of age and 80.6 million adolescents and young adults 15 to 24 years of age had obesity, affecting approximately 160 million children and adolescents worldwide. This number is expected to grow to more than 250 million by 2030 (10, 11). Many countries are increasingly facing a double burden of underweight and obesity, largely driven by rising obesity rates in both adult and paediatric populations. Regardless of income level, countries are also confronted with varying obesity rates within their borders. These variations are largely reflective of poverty, education, access to health care, and other determinants of health (10, 11). Furthermore, a 2024 systematic review and meta-analysis reported the global obesity prevalence among children and adolescents to be 8.5%, noting significant increases over the past two decades (13). Regional variations are notable: the highest paediatric obesity rates are observed in North America (~17%), the Caribbean (~19%), Central America (~16%), and Polynesia (~20%). Conversely, lower childhood obesity rates are seen in Central Africa (~2%), Eastern and Western Africa (~4%), Southern Africa (~6%), as well as in Western, Eastern, and Northern Europe (~5%) (13). Although factors such as diet, physical activity, and personal behaviour have frequently been cited, there is no clear consensus on the exact causes behind the rise in obesity rates over recent decades. Multisectoral and policy efforts have for the most part failed to stem obesogenic environments, address drivers of obesity, and remove blame from people with obesity and their families (14). Commercial determinants of health, for example private sector actions around the production of industrialized products currently known as ultra-processed foods and other unhealthy food options, marketing of unhealthy commodities, and the promotion of sedentary lifestyles, have a profound impact on people’s behaviour regarding food choices, physical activity, and consuming practices (15). Among other related actions, WHO advocates for marketing restrictions for foods and beverages with respect to children and for breast-milk substitutes (16). Importantly, recent evidence syntheses suggest that while combined dietary and physical activity interventions may offer some benefits, their impact on preventing obesity in children and adolescents remains limited. The risk of serious adverse events from these interventions appears to be low, yet the overall quality of evidence supporting these findings is also low (17, 18, 19). Physical activity contributes significantly to bone and skeletal development, cardiometabolic health, and the improvement of cognitive and motor skills in children and adolescents. It also reduces the risk of obesity and chronic diseases later in life. Alarmingly, as of 2024, 80% of adolescents were not meeting WHO’s recommended physical activity levels (20, 21). Moreover, rising screen time has contributed to increased sedentary behaviour and a surge in mental health issues in children, both of which are linked to obesity (3, 4).

In terms of diet, major economic and technological transformations have driven a global shift from subsistence and local food systems to commercially driven food production and an increase in marketing industrialized products currently known as ultra-processed foods. This transition has increased the intake of calories, sugars, processed foods, and animal-source foods, especially in low-income countries, while reducing food preparation time and decreasing the consumption of fibre-rich and fresh foods (22, 23). Societal and environmental conditions strongly influence individual choices around food and physical activity. This perspective is essential, as obesity is often wrongly framed purely as a matter of personal responsibility. While multimodal interventions may help boost physical activity and improve dietary habits (24), addressing obesity effectively requires coordinated, multisectoral actions. Such strategies should focus on improving access to healthy foods and safe physical activity spaces, addressing poverty, and reforming food production, marketing, and pricing systems (25). Rationale WHO’s commitment to addressing the current global trends in obesity epidemiology and to strengthening existing prevention-oriented strategies motivated the development of relevant normative guidance. The present document presents guidance for the people-centred integrated management and care of children between 29 days and under 10 years of age with obesity, using a primary health care approach. “People-centred” entails incorporating the perspectives, preferences, and values of children and those affected by the recommendations (26). Dietary interventions: An approach to creating combinations of calories, macro and/or micronutrients for obesity management. Dietary approaches and food composition depend primarily on the child’s age, weight loss goal, coexisting conditions and food preferences and habits (51). Physical activity: Any bodily movement produced by skeletal muscles that requires energy expenditure (44, 45, 46). Regular physical activity can prevent and contribute to the management of type 2 diabetes, heart disease, hypertension, some types of cancer, and other chronic diseases (52, 53, 54). The 2020 WHO guidelines on physical activity and sedentary behaviour provide physical activity recommendations for people irrespective of gender, cultural background or socioeconomic status (3, 4). Behaviour changing interventions: Interventions designed to effect individual behaviour change (55, 56). They play a role in obesity management by addressing the energy-balance behaviours that contribute to weight gain, and that hamper obesity management efforts. While there is no consensus on a taxonomy or classification, behaviour changing interventions may include (i) interventions that invoke behavioural models or theory; (ii) interventions based on psychotherapeutic models such as behavioural therapy, cognitive behavioural therapies, mindfulness, attitude and relationship techniques, psychotherapy, psychodynamic therapies, humanistic therapies, stimulus control, goal setting, and self-monitoring; and (iii) multimodal interventions, which are composite approaches to modifying behaviour using more than one component (55, 56). Multimodal interventions: Therapeutic approaches that incorporate a combination of different methods, strategies, or modes of treatment. Instead of relying on a single approach, multimodal interventions utilize a variety of components, for example behavioural and educational interventions, dietary management, and encouragement to increase physical activity and reduce sedentary behaviour. In this guideline, pharmacological and/or bariatric surgery interventions were not considered as components of multimodal interventions (57). Digital health interventions: A discrete functionality of digital technology that is applied to achieve health objectives. Digital health interventions may be implemented within digital health applications, and information and communication technology systems, including

<!-- PDF page 35 -->

communication channels (58). They include the use of wearable or implantable devices to monitor diet and physical activity, websites, and text messages or other texting. There are also mobile applications to deliver information on diet and physical activity and monitor behaviours. Other interventions include exergaming or active video gaming to promote physical activity, as well as telemedicine to provide counselling and monitor interventions. Pharmacological interventions: These aim to reduce energy intake and/or increase energy expenditure. They may be a suitable course of action for obesity management when lifestyle interventions have failed and there are serious coexisting conditions that affect physical and mental well-being (59). However, patients need to be closely monitored for potential adverse events (60). Currently, the United States Food and Drug Administration (FDA) and the European Medicines Agency (EMA) have approved orlistat, semaglutide, and liraglutide for the treatment of obesity in adolescents. The FDA has also approved phentermine and phentermine/ topiramate. These pharmacological interventions are to be administered as an adjunct to diet, physical activity, and other lifestyle modifications (61). Metabolic and bariatric surgery: Surgical procedures to manage obesity and obesity-related conditions. The rationale of metabolic and bariatric surgery is to restrict food intake and reduce the absorption of food. Different types of procedures are available, including Roux-en-Y gastric bypass, adjustable banding, and sleeve gastrectomy (6 2, 63, 64, 65). Weight-loss or weight-management devices: Minimally invasive interventions for obesity management. They include weight-loss devices such as intragastric balloons, electrical stimulation, gastric emptying systems; and weight-maintenance devices such as removable oral palatal space-occupying devices, and ingested, transient, space-occupying devices (66, 67). In addition, the drafting of recommendations considered the following principles: Primary health care approach: A whole-of-society approach to health that aims to maximize the level and distribution of health and well-being through three components: 1) primary care and essential public health functions as the core of integrated health services; 2) addressing the broader determinants of health through multisectoral policy and action; and 3) empowering individuals, families and communities to take charge of their own health. The function of primary health care is to coordinate the care of people and their health needs over the life course and across the continuum of care, in primary care facilities and communities, and for individuals and populations. Care needs to be comprehensive, coordinated, people-centred, continuous and accessible (31, 70). Integrated Management of Childhood Illness (IMCI): IMCI includes three components: 1) improving case management skills of health workers, 2) improving overall health systems, and 3) improving family and community practices (30, 7 1). In health care facilities, IMCI entails the identification of illness in outpatient settings, and adopting a people-centred, integrated care perspective rather than focusing on a specific illness. It also entails working with caregivers, and expediting the referral of severely ill children to the appropriate facility or specialist in the health system or network of service providers to address the relevant health needs (36). People-centred care: An approach to care that purposely adopts the perspectives of individuals, carers, families and communities as participants in and beneficiaries of trusted health systems that respond to their needs and preferences in humane and holistic ways. People-centred care also requires that people have the education and support they need to make decisions and participate in their own care (26). Compassionate care: A principled and intentional effort to understand a person, discern their needs, and alleviate their suffering through relational understanding and action. Compassionate care in obesity management requires a competency-based approach where health workers adopt non-judgmental and non-stigmatizing language, attitudes, knowledge, and skills that cover the multiple domains of compassion, as well as assessments by or feedback from patient’s families or peers (27, 28, 29). Disability: The experience of any condition that makes it more difficult for a person to do certain activities or have equitable access within a given society. Disabilities may be cognitive, developmental, intellectual, mental, physical, sensory, or a combination of multiple factors (9, 32). Equity: Equity refers to a condition in which society is characterized by justice, equality, impartiality and fairness, including fair and equal distribution of power, economic resources, opportunities, goods and services across the social spectrum. In an equitable society people are not excluded from the activities of society, such as education, employment, or health care and there is no bias or favouritism (32, 33). Gender equality: Requires the equal enjoyment by women, men, girls, boys and gender minorities of socially valued goods, opportunities, resources and rewards. It means that all persons, regardless of their gender, enjoy the same status in society; have the same entitlements to all human rights; enjoy the same level of respect in the community; can take advantage of the same opportunities to make choices about their lives; and have the same amount of power to shape the outcomes of these choices (32, 33).

In all their diversity: Recognizing and accepting individual differences such as gender, nationality, race, ethnicity, ability, sexual orientation, socio-economic status, religious beliefs, political beliefs, or other ideologies (32, 33). Multidisciplinary teams: Various health workers collaborating to provide a broad range of services in a coordinated approach. The composition of multidisciplinary teams in primary care will vary by setting but may include generalist medical practitioners (including family doctors and general practitioners), physician assistants, nurses, specialist nurses, community health workers, pharmacists, social workers, dieticians, mental health counsellors, physiotherapists, patient educators, managers, support staff, and other primary care specialists. According to the WHO Handbook for guideline development, a strong recommendation is based on the confidence that the desirable effects associated with a recommendation outweigh the undesirable effects. Most people would want the recommended intervention, and only a small proportion would not. Furthermore, the recommendation can be adopted as policy and as a quality or performance indicator. For a conditional recommendation, there is less certainty on the balance between the desirable and undesirable effects. While many people would want the recommended intervention, many others would not. Southcombe et al. conducted a systematic review including 125 studies with participants 2 to 18 years of age. This review found a gradient effect according to the energy target of different dietary types. It reported that very low-energy diets were associated with a reduction of 4.40 kg/m in BMI (n = 3; 95% confidence interval (CI) –7.01 to –1.79), while in contrast, dietary interventions with no specified energy target were associated with a BMI gain of 0.17 kg/m (n = 22; 95% CI 0.05 to 0.40) (83). ## Recommendations

<!-- PDF page 44 -->

Gow et al. 2014 found a greater reduction in BMI immediately post-intervention associated with a low-carbohydrate diet in children with overweight or obesity 6 to 14 years of age. However, the quality of the studies was limited and cardiometabolic benefits were inconsistent (85). Six trials compared high protein diets (19–30% energy) to isocaloric standard-protein diets (15–20% energy), and one compared a high fat diet (40% energy) to an isocaloric standard-fat diet (27% energy). There were no differences in BMI in these studies. Results suggested that improved weight status can be achieved in children with overweight or obesity irrespective of the macronutrient distribution of a reduced-energy diet (85). Current evidence, some very recent, suggests that dietary interventions integrated into multimodal lifestyle programmes generally produce modest weight reductions over short- to long-term periods (86). There is increasing interest in exploring novel strategies for obesity management, including but not limited to gut microbiome and personalized nutrition interventions. Obesity risk assessments based on anthropometric measures and/or in combination with child, parental, and community characteristics, all of which can be readily available in health care and community settings, have low accuracy for broad-scale screening on the risk of childhood obesity, thus hampering potential societal-level individual preventive measures (87). Gut microbiome interventions may influence obesity by altering the diversity and abundance of intestinal bacteria. This effect could be mediated through compounds ingested that affect small intestine function, changes in substrate availability affecting colonic microbial composition, and microbial metabolites influencing metabolism (88). A 2025 Cochrane systematic review analysed 17 RCTs involving 838 participants across various countries. These trials assessed interventions such as prebiotics, probiotics, synbiotics, short-chain fatty acids, and faecal microbiota transplantation. Overall, authors found that these interventions resulted in little to no effect in obesity-related outcomes (89). Personalized nutrition interventions tailor dietary approaches based on characteristics related to individual biology and environment rather than relying on uniform “one-size-fits-all” strategies. These personalized approaches consider factors such as genetic differences, social environments, circadian rhythms, and psychosocial characteristics to optimize nutrition and potentially improve obesity management (90). The effects of these interventions in children and adolescents with obesity remain uncertain. A 2025 Cochrane systematic review identified three relevant studies involving 105 participants; authors reported that the effect of personalized nutrition on body weight or BMI in children and adolescents was uncertain (91).

According to WHO and FAO, a healthy diet needs to be adequate, balanced, moderate and diverse. Notably, there is no direct evidence on children between 0 and 6 years of age, and no direct evidence on medications other than metformin on children between 0 and 9 years of age (167). Seventeen of the 37 studies were completed in high-income countries, six in middle-income countries and three in low-income countries. The remaining were multicentre trials completed in different countries (167). The length of treatment ranged from three to 16 months, and the length of follow-up ranged from six to 31 months, with a median of 11 months. Pharmacotherapy was part of multimodal strategies of care, added to baseline treatments administered to both the intervention and control groups (Annex 2. Pharmacotherapy). Out of the 37 studies, 31 compared pharmacological interventions to placebo. Pharmacological interventions (glucagon-like peptide-1 receptor agonists (GLP-1 RA), metformin, orlistat, sibutramine, topiramate, phentermine and topiramate) found a mean difference (MD) BMI change in favour of pharmacological interventions of -1.80 kg/m (95% CI -2.36 to -1.24) (25 studies; 3091 participants; low-certainty evidence) and a MD weight change from baseline in favour of pharmacological interventions of -5.47 kg (95% CI -7.45 to -3.5) (20 trials; 2380 participants; low-certainty evidence) (Annex 2, Summary of findings table 7.1). The remaining six studies compared pharmacological interventions, specifically metformin, to no treatment. These had a very limited number of participants and reported results similar to those for pharmacological interventions compared to placebo (Annex 2. Summary of findings table 7.2).

<!-- PDF page 57 -->

Previous systematic reviews focused primarily on three interventions: metformin, sibutramine, and orlistat. Overall, the findings of these reviews for BMI and weight change were similar to the findings of this review (127, 176 - 184). Cochrane had published a systematic review on pharmacological interventions for obesity management in children and adolescents (176). Instead of updating this review, WHO commissioned the new Cochrane systematic review reported above, including new outcomes and subgroup analyses as advised during consultations with the general public and with the Guideline Development Group. Besides these modifications, the WHO-commissioned Cochrane systematic review included new pharmacological interventions, notably GLP-1 RA. A recent RCT included in the WHO-commissioned systematic review provides further evidence of anti-obesity medications among children. A 2025 phase 3a study evaluated once-daily subcutaneous liraglutide at a dose of 3.0 mg (or the highest tolerated dose) compared to placebo, combined with lifestyle interventions in children aged 6 to under 12 years with obesity. After 56 weeks, the liraglutide group showed a mean BMI reduction of 5.8% from the baseline, compared to a 1.6% increase in the placebo group. Adverse events occurred in 89% of participants in both groups (50 out of 56 in the liraglutide arm and 23 out of 26 in the placebo arm) most of which were mild or moderate in severit (171). Three recently published evidence syntheses provide further insight into the effect of anti- obesity medications among children and adolescents. A 2025 systematic review and network meta-analysis (185) and a 2024 systematic review (186) reported that anti-obesity medications led to improvements in anthropometric and cardiometabolic health outcomes. These reviews also reported that long-term effects and potential adverse events remain unclear. Relatedly, a 2023 assessment of undesired effects of GLP-1 RAs on children and adolescents revealed that GLP-1 RAs may lead to inappropriate reductions in energy intake. Additional concerns include potential misuse among patients with eating disorders or athletes (187). A 2024 research letter assessed the dispensing of GLP-1 RA medications to adolescents and young adults aged 12 to 25 years between 2020 to 2023; this was based on data from a private database that captured prescriptions from over 93% of US retail pharmacies. GLP-1 RA dispensing surged by nearly 600% in this age group compared to an approximate 3% reduction of other medications (188). An additional consideration related to pharmacotherapy is the risk of weight gain associated with some medications prescribed for treating conditions other than obesity. A 2024 literature review examined the physiology and mechanisms behind weight gain and metabolic dysregulation associated with medications prescribed for both obesity-related and unrelated conditions. Health workers should remain aware of the possible side effects of antipsychotics, glucocorticoids, beta-adrenergic blockers, tricyclic antidepressants, antihistamines, insulin, neuropathic agents, sleep aids, and steroids, which were identified as pharmacological contributors to weight gain (189). Lastly, health workers should be aware of potential genetic, hereditary, and familial factors (73, 74).

## Recommendation

WHO does not recommend pharmacological interventions for obesity management for children 0–9 years of age. Strength of the recommendation: Strong. Quality of the evidence: Low.

## Justification

The GDG agreed on a strong recommendation because the WHO-commissioned Cochrane systematic review of RCTs assessing the effect of any form of pharmacological interventions compared to no intervention or placebo for obesity in children and adolescents did not identify any studies that included children under 6 years of age. There is a need for high-quality evidence on the long-term benefits and harms of the different drugs, different doses, and/or their active ingredients for obesity management in eligible populations. ## Recommendation

WHO recommends a structured multimodal intervention including a combination of diet, physical activity, and behavioural components for the integrated management of children with obesity*. It should consider age, gender, health status, food preferences, nutritional education, physical fitness, cultural context, and the child’s interests, values and preferences as well as resources and support of parents and caregivers. It should be delivered and supervised by health workers with knowledge on nutrition, physical activity, and behaviour changing interventions, and involve parents and/or caregivers.

* Other components of multimodal interventions include changes in sleep behaviour and in

sedentary behaviour patterns, and the use of digital technologies. Multimodal interventions could involve family, friends, caregivers and schools; improve access to care; and promote awareness and educational campaigns for children, caregivers, schools and communities. Strength of the recommendation: Strong. Quality of the evidence: Very low.

## Justification

The GDG considered this recommendation to be strong because children with obesity and their parents value the perceived benefits associated with a healthy diet and staying active, including weight loss, social acceptance, and self-esteem (Annex 3. Evidence-to-decision considerations for multimodal interventions). Even though the benefits are small, the balance between benefits and harms still favours the intervention. Multimodal interventions are likely to be acceptable and feasible if they are appropriate for age, gender, and ethnicity; if they are personalized; and if they acknowledge parental financial situation and schedules. Relatedly, the GDG acknowledged that multimodal interventions may incur costs on families and that the effect on equity is unclear. Underserved communities, girls, and minoritized ethnic groups are less likely to have access (Annex 3. Evidence-to-decision considerations for multimodal interventions). The WHO-commissioned review retrieved RCTs of multimodal interventions delivered either in healthcare settings or in community settings, for example, schools, churches, and community centres. Interventions consisted of dietary modi fi cation, physical activity, and behavioural modifications compared to usual care. Results were similar in both settings, including small effects on BMI, physical well-being, and quality of life.

## Remarks

The recommendations on diet, physical activity and behavioural components established during this guideline development process could form the basis of a multimodal intervention. This guideline includes recommendations on sleep behaviour, sedentary behaviour, digital technologies, and the need for involving family, friends, caregivers, schools and the community (57). The nature of the intervention can be decided based on the results of a detailed initial clinical assessment that considers anthropometric measures as well as a comprehensive family and medical history, a review of psychosocial and behavioural factors (such as eating habits, physical activity, and sleep routines), and relevant laboratory tests where appropriate (75). Parental involvement can enhance the effectiveness of lifestyle interventions. Parents can support the child throughout the intervention, change their own behaviours, and adapt parenting styles.

<!-- PDF page 63 -->

Multimodal interventions may be cost-effective due to potential benefits in weight management and the prevention of chronic diseases. Referral from the primary health care setting to specialized care may be required, including notification of other health workers and clear, compassionate communication with the family (75). The involvement of paediatric obesity experts, secondary or specialized care, and/or multidisciplinary teams in multimodal lifestyle interventions could be an option. While there is no ideal follow-up interval for children with obesity, the frequency of visits should consider the child’s overall clinical condition, coexisting conditions, medication use, and social vulnerability (75). Whole-of-society approaches addressing the causes of inequities in access are required to enhance participation in obesity management interventions. Support from governments, schools, and the community could facilitate the implementation of multimodal interventions.

## Research gaps

Research on multimodal interventions in children should prioritize well-designed and well- reported RCTs and prospective controlled cohort studies reporting efficacy, effectiveness, safety profile, long-term secondary effects, weight regain, and cost. Multimodal interventions need to consider the specific barriers that children face in order to prevent inequities in obesity health outcomes. Studies on cost-effectiveness analysis of multimodal interventions for children with obesity. Studies on the cost or resources use of multimodal interventions in children with obesity.

## Summary of judgements

The GDG agreed that the benefits and harms for both healthcare-based and community-based multimodal interventions are small. The balance of desirable and undesirable effects likely favours the intervention, with very low-quality evidence. The GDG voted that this recommendation should be strong, and highlighted that there is probably no important variability in how children and their parents value the main outcomes. Children prefer to participate in multimodal interventions because they value the perceived benefits associated with a healthy diet and staying active, including weight loss, social acceptance, and self-esteem. Parents and children value interventions that include supportive interactions, and that focus not only on weight loss but also on changing behaviour and improving self-esteem (92, 131). Multimodal interventions are likely to be acceptable and feasible. Multimodal interventions that are personalized and that incorporate supportive interactions with health workers and schools are more acceptable (96, 113). Support from stakeholders such as governments, teachers and other school staff, as well as the community could foster feasibility. Costs are unclear; families may incur costs associated with the components of multimodal interventions including diet, exercise, and behaviour change. Based on indirect evidence form adolescents, the GDG agreed that the intervention is likely to be cost-effective. Effects on health equity are unclear. Underserved communities, girls, and minoritized ethnic groups are at a higher risk of obesity and are less likely to participate in obesity management interventions (122, 130, 131) (Annex 3. Evidence-to-decision considerations for multimodal interventions).

Annex1. Background questions Genetic or familial factors

## Question 1. Are there genetic, hereditary, and familial factors that need to be considered

when managing children with obesity? Obesity during early childhood results from a combination of genetic, environmental, and developmental influences. Research has identified over one hundred genetic locations, predominantly within 12 to 15 key genes, that are consistently linked with obesity in children (7 3). Many of these genetic variants are also associated with obesity in adulthood, indicating that inherited influences on body composition tend to remain stable throughout life. Notably studied genes include FTO–especially the SNP rs9939609, which has been linked to modest increases in BMI and replicated in populations across Europe, Asia, and the Americas–as well as MC4R and several others involved in regulating appetite and energy balance, such as TMEM18, SEC16B, ADCY3, BDNF, and NEGR1 (73). While researchers have explored gene-gene interactions and developed genetic risk scores, results have so far been inconclusive. These observations support the view that obesity is polygenic, involving many genes with small effects that interact with environmental factors (73). Early-life environmental factors can also affect how genes are expressed through epigenetic changes. Among these, DNA methylation has been the most thoroughly investigated mechanism, commonly assessed using blood or cord blood samples. Some studies have linked specific CpG sites with future obesity risk (74). Circulating microRNAs (miRNAs) also show altered expression patterns in children with obesity, though these findings are inconsistent due to differences in study design and the biological samples analysed. Despite growing insights, significant gaps remain, particularly in understanding how environmental agents, such as endocrine-disrupting chemicals (EDCs) influence epigenetic mechanisms associated with obesity development (74). Initial clinical assessment

## Question 2. What are the components of an initial clinical assessment* when managing

children with obesity?

* Besides anthropometry and physical examination, an initial clinical assessment may

include family and psycho-social history, behaviour (such as diet, physical activity, sleep) and biochemical parameters. When evaluating children with overweight and obesity, the initial clinical assessment should go beyond measuring height and weight and include a detailed family and medical history, a review of psychosocial and behavioural factors (such as eating habits, physical activity, sleep routines), and relevant laboratory tests where appropriate (75). There should be appropriate physical space and including larger equipment (for example, examination table, chair, blood pressure cuffs).

<!-- PDF page 88 -->

Taking a comprehensive history from the caregiver is essential to guide treatment and build a positive, trusting relationship. This discussion should be age-appropriate, respectful, and culturally sensitive, using open-ended questions that encourage caregiver input. It is important to assess factors like the child’s usual diet (including portion sizes and frequency of high-sugar or processed foods), time spent being physically active versus sedentary, and sleep patterns. Family history plays an important role and should include any cases of obesity, type 2 diabetes, or gestational diabetes during the patient’s mother’s pregnancy. Health workers should also ask about long-term medication use (such as corticosteroids) and psychosocial issues that may impact the child’s well-being, such as family stress or limited access to healthy food and safe play environments (75). The physical exam should be complete and gentle, covering the child’s growth, development, vital signs, and body systems. Key signs to check include high blood pressure, signs of insulin resistance like darkened skin patches (acanthosis nigricans), or physical features suggestive of hormonal issues (for example, moon face, or central fat accumulation). Height, weight, BMI- for-age, waist circumference and other anthropometric measures as well as developmental milestones should all be recorded, with referrals made for further assessment if needed (7 5). Follow-up

## Question 3. What is the optimal frequency of follow-up (for example, intensive follow-up

(three months or less) compared to non-intensive follow-up (over three months) ) in the clinical setting for the integrated management of children with obesity? Are there specific indications according to sex/gender or disability? How often should anthropometric, dietary and physical activity measurements be monitored and tracked in the clinical setting? In the context of ongoing care for obesity and chronic conditions, there is no single ideal follow-up schedule for children. The frequency of follow-up should be based on the child’s clinical condition, presence of other health problems, any prescribed medications, and whether the visit was routine or in response to new or worsening symptoms. Health workers should guide caregivers to return for a follow-up visit after a specific number of days and to check the child’s progress or response to treatment, which may be before the scheduled follow up visit (75). Although no fixed schedule is determined by sex, care should be inclusive and age appropriate. Growth monitoring tools, including weight, length/height, and BMI-for-age, are available and should be used separately for boys and girls. It is also important to assess other influences on the child’s health, including family stress, caregiver mental health, food insecurity, or exposure to violence or neglect. Children with complex care needs, such as those with a combination of chronic health conditions, developmental delays, or social vulnerabilities, require coordinated and individualized follow-up. Flexible communication options, such as caregiver phone check-ins or digital reminders, may help support ongoing follow-up and reinforce healthy routines, especially for families living in remote or underserved areas (75).

Referral

## Question 4. What are the criteria for a referral to specialized obesity management care*

when managing children with obesity?

* Specialized obesity management care including but not limited to endocrinologist,

dietitian, clinical nutritionist, paediatrician, psychologist, medical laboratory technologist, or another member of a multidisciplinary team. Referral to specialized services should be considered when initial efforts, such as improvements in diet and increase in physical activity, do not lead to progress, or when a child has obesity- related health conditions that need more advanced care. This may include referral to nutritionists, psychologists, or paediatric endocrinologists (75). If the cause of obesity appears to be complex or beyond lifestyle factors, specialist evaluation is recommended. When referring a child to specialist services or hospital care, it is important to communicate clearly and compassionately with the family. Caregivers should be informed about the reason for the referral, what to expect during the visit, and how the care team will support the child. Offering reassurance is important: families should know that the referral will provide access to appropriate expertise, diagnostic tools, and trained staff. A written referral should include a summary of the child’s medical background, reasons for referral, and previous interventions. This helps ensure coordinated and effective care in the next stage of evaluation or treatment (7 5). Gender- or sex-specific considerations

## Question 5. Are there any gender- or sex-specific aspects that need to be considered

when managing children with obesity in the primary health care setting? There are important sex- and gender-related factors to consider when managing obesity in children in primary health care. Boys and girls may experience and respond to obesity differently. For example, girls may be more affected by how others see their bodies and may become upset or worried about their weight, which can lead to eating disorders or to avoid being active. Boys may be less likely to talk about how they feel or ask for help, even if they are struggling emotionally or socially because of their weight (33, 76, 77). Health workers should be aware of these differences and use a gender-sensitive approach when working with children and families. This means using kind, respectful words, helping all children feel safe and included, and designing activities that every child can enjoy. For example, physical activity programmes at school or in the community should make sure girls feel welcome and safe to join, especially in places where girls may have fewer chances to play or be active (33, 76, 77). Sex and gender also interact with other factors like poverty, disability, and background. For instance, girls in poor households may have fewer healthy diet or physical activity options, and children with disabilities may have a harder time joining in physical activities or getting regular health care due to barriers in the surrounding environment (33, 76, 77). Disability-specific considerations

## Question 6. Are there any disability*-specific aspects that need to be considered when

managing children with obesity in the primary health care setting?

* Disability refers to the outcome of the interaction between health conditions and

impairments that a child might have with personal and environmental factors. Children with obesity and disability often require personalized attention, as their underlying impairments or health conditions can significantly impact their growth, development, and daily life. For example, some underlying impairments or health conditions may affect eating behaviours and energy balance, and when coupled with barriers in the surrounding environment, they can affect their ability to participate in activities with peers. These challenges can also place emotional and financial stress on families and caregivers, especially when frequent medical care is needed (32, 78, 79). Primary care providers should ensure that assessments and care plans are adapted to the child’s functional abilities and communication needs. Standard growth monitoring tools may not be appropriate for all children with disabilities, and interventions should be flexible and family-centred. Using frameworks like the WHO’s International Classification of Functioning (ICF) can support a more holistic understanding of each child’s needs (32, 78, 79). Ultimately, inclusive care that addresses both medical and social aspects -while involving caregivers as active partners- is essential for supporting healthy growth and well-being in children with disabilities and obesity (32, 78).

Mental health considerations

## Question 7. What are the approaches for treating and/or co-managing mental health

conditions* in children with obesity? Should considerations regarding how mental health issues can exacerbate obesity or vice versa be explored – and if yes, starting at what age?

* WHO conceptualizes mental health as a “state of well-being in which the individual

realizes his or her own abilities, can cope with the normal stresses of life, can work productively and fruitfully, and is able to make a contribution to his or her community”. Mental health conditions include but are not limited to eating disorders, anxiety, stress, depression, isolation, suicidal ideation, weight-based stigmatisation and victimisation, low self-esteem and poor body image. Addressing mental health is essential when treating children with obesity, and the relationship between mental health conditions and obesity should be considered very early and at every stage of care. Mental health issues such as anxiety, depression, low self-esteem, and emotional dysregulation can contribute to unhealthy eating behaviours, physical inactivity, sleep disturbances, and social withdrawal, all of which increase the risk of obesity. Conversely, children with obesity may experience stigma, bullying, or exclusion, which can negatively impact their emotional well-being, forming a bidirectional relationship between obesity and mental health (80). Effective management in primary care requires an integrated and family-centred approach. This includes routine screening for emotional and behavioural issues using developmentally appropriate tools, providing psychoeducation to caregivers, and promoting nurturing caregiver-child relationships. Early interventions, such as caregiver support and emotional regulation strategies, are recommended as part of holistic obesity care even in younger children as these years are critical for shaping self-image, eating behaviours, and coping skills. Approaches should also be inclusive and responsive to children with neurodevelopmental conditions or disabilities, who may face additional emotional and behavioural challenges and are more likely to experience obesity. In these cases, care plans should be individualized and coordinated with mental health, educational, and social services. Psychological interventions such as cognitive behavioural strategies, play-based therapy, and caregiver-led support programmes have shown promise in improving outcomes for both mental health and weight-related concerns (80). In summary, mental health and obesity in children are deeply interconnected. Primary care settings should address both conditions early and together, using inclusive, developmentally appropriate, and family-engaged strategies to improve long-term health and well-being. Provider competencies

## Question 8. What is the competency-based training (required knowledge, attitude and

skills) that primary health care workers should receive to manage children with obesity for improved health, functioning and obesity-associated disability? Primary health care services are essential for recognizing, managing, and coordinating care for children with obesity at an early stage. To help health workers provide high-quality, child- focused care, a set of core competencies has been outlined based on current evidence about the roles and training needs of primary care providers. These competencies are grouped into four main areas (8 1): 1. Medical knowledge, patient care, and procedural skills: Emphasizes accurate use of BMI charts, percentiles and growth charts, early recognition of obesity-related comorbidities, and adherence to evidence-based treatment guidelines. Health workers should provide developmentally appropriate care and refer patients to specialists when necessary. 2. Interpersonal and communication skills: Highlights the use of empathetic, non-stigmatizing and non-judgemental communication, motivational interviewing, and culturally responsive counselling. It also stresses the importance of engaging families, recognizing social determinants of health, and connecting patients with community-based supports. 3. Practice-based learning, improvement, and professionalism: Underscores the need to incorporate obesity-specific competencies into pre-service education and continuing professional development. Training should address provider bias, developmental considerations, and include regular assessment of competency. 4. Systems-based practice: Promotes interdisciplinary collaboration, timely referrals, and integration of obesity care into clinical workflows and electronic health records. It calls for enabling policies, sustainable resources, and scalable, context-sensitive strategies to support implementation across diverse health systems. These competencies enable primary care providers to: (1) evaluate and manage obesity in children using safe, evidence-based, and age-appropriate approaches; (2) communicate clearly and compassionately to involve both the child’s caregivers and the child in care decisions; (3) work closely with specialists to ensure children receive well-coordinated and complete care; and (4) engage in ongoing learning and skill development to consistently deliver high-quality obesity care (11).

Evidence-to-decision considerations for dietary interventions

## Question 1. Dietary interventions

In children with obesity (P), what is the effect of dietary intervention (I) compared to no intervention (C) in the clinical setting for the integrated management of obesity to improve health, functioning and to reduce obesity-associated disability (O)?

## Evidence-to-decision considerations

Benefits The GDG agreed that the benefits of dietary interventions alone were small and variable, depending on the type of dietary intervention. Harms GDG members highlighted that the risk for adverse events is likely small. However, high-protein or ketogenic diet intake may result in undesirable outcomes, for example, increases in BMI. Certainty of evidence The certainty of evidence for critical outcomes was very low. Values and Children with obesity valued the potential benefits of dietary preferences approaches aimed at promoting healthy food choices (96). The GDG highlighted that children and their parents valued interventions that involve the entire family unit, with fun and hands-on activities. Schools play a key role in ensuring coordinated activities and consistent messaging (9 2, 96). Balance between The GDG considered that the balance between benefits and harms benefits and harms probably favours the intervention. Resources The GDG highlighted that financial constraints may hinder parents from adhering to intervention-specific food or meal plans (9 2, 9 8). There is a need for nutrition knowledge and various abilities, such as cooking skills. Cost-effectiveness No studies addressed the issue of cost-effectiveness for dietary interventions.

<!-- PDF page 126 -->

Equity Cultural, socioeconomic, and environmental factors, including the school environment, intertwine to influence dietary behaviour, affecting both immediate choices and long-term health outcomes (99, 100). Gender, culture, ethnicity, socioeconomic status, and parental input critically influence diet and the corresponding health outcomes (101 - 10 7). Early education and the environment play crucial roles in children’s long-term health. Acceptability Dietary interventions were more likely to be acceptable when they were personalized, age-appropriate, and culturally sensitive. Feasibility Feasibility varies depending on cost and knowledge required to prepare healthy meals and caregiver involvement, among other variables (9 2, 9 7, 9 8). ## Question 4. Sleep

Should children with obesity (P), improve sleep and/or sleep hygiene (I) in different duration, frequencies, or patterns as measured by objective and subjective methods (C) for improved health, functioning and reduced obesity-associated disability (O) for the integrated management of obesity?

## Evidence-to-decision considerations

Benefits The GDG considered that, although small, the benefits of physical activity are clear for all ages and health statuses. Harms Undesirable effects are likely to be trivial to small. To avoid physical or mental harm, the type, intensity and duration of physical activity should consider age, physical and health status. Certainty of evidence The certainty of evidence for critical outcomes was very low. Values and The GDG determined that there is probably no important variability preferences in how much children value physical activity. Parents and children value informal and fun activities while considering safety and weather conditions (9 2, 1 13).

<!-- PDF page 127 -->

Balance between According to the GDG, the balance between benefits and harms benefits and harms probably favours physical activity. Resources The GDG agreed that costs vary. Access to parks, athletic facilities, and recreation areas may be provided at low or no cost. Structured physical activity intervention programmes, such as school-based programmes, or programmes led or prescribed by health workers may increase costs. Cost-effectiveness Physical activity interventions may be cost-effective due to potential benefits of physical activity in weight management and prevention of chronic diseases. Economic analyses reported that physical activity programmes are cost-effective due to large costs of physical inactivity (114). They also result in potential benefits of physical activity in obesity management and prevention of chronic diseases (115 - 121). Equity Worldwide, women, minoritized ethnic groups, disadvantaged communities, and individuals living with disabilities or chronic diseases are more prone to inactivity (122). Access to safe, affordable, and suitable programmes and spaces for physical activity may increase equity if access to all is ensured, considering that children in low-income settings and those with disabilities may experience barriers to accessing opportunities to engage in physical activity. Acceptability Children and caregivers favoured interventions that incorporate fun, informal (non-competitive) physical activity and that use technology (9 2, 1 13). Feasibility The GDG underscored that feasibility relies on the provision of safe spaces, equipment, and facilities at different setting at low or no cost. ## Evidence-to-decision considerations

Benefits Digital interventions in combination with conventional care or alone, may lead to small benefits. Evidence on children is limited; most studies include either adolescents alone or a combination or children and adolescents without providing disaggregated data. Harms Undesirable effects are unknown.

Indirect evidence from adolescents reported that people valued the effectiveness of digital health interventions, but they were also concerned over data security (160 - 16 3). Balance between The GDG voted that the balance of desirable and undesirable effects benefits and harms likely favours the intervention. Resources Costs vary depending on various factors such as delivery format, development process, components of intervention, workers involved, and settings. Cost-effectiveness There were no direct data on cost-effectiveness. Indirect data from adolescents suggests that digital health interventions could be less costly and more effective compared to no intervention. Equity The GDG highlighted that impact on equity varies. ## Question 11. Multimodal interventions

In children with obesity (P), what is the effect of multimodal interventions delivered by a multidisciplinary team (I) compared to standard care (C) in the clinical setting for the integrated management of obesity to improve health, functioning and reduce obesity- associated disability (O)?

## Evidence-to-decision considerations

Benefits The GDG considered that the benefits of multimodal interventions, based either in health care settings or in community settings, were small. Harms In general, the risk of adverse events may be low. Certainty of evidence The certainty of evidence was very low. Annex 3 105 105

<!-- PDF page 132 -->

Values and There is probably no important variability in values and preferences. preferences Parents and children value interventions including supportive interactions and focused not only on weight loss but also behaviour change and self-esteem improvement. Children prefer participating in multimodal interventions because they value the perceived benefits associated with a healthy diet and staying active, including weight loss, social acceptance, and self-esteem. Parents and children value interventions including supportive interactions and focus not only on weight loss but also behaviour change and self-esteem improvement (9 2, 1 31).
